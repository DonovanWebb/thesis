\section{\textbf{Hybrid Spin-Oscillator Control in Trapped Ions}}
\label{sec:CVQC}
CV quantum computation~\cite{lloyd_quantum_1999} offers an alternative strategy for encoding quantum information, utilising
quantum harmonic oscillators, termed \emph{qumodes},
as the fundamental quantum object, rather than discrete variable (DV) systems, e.g. the two-level qubit. A single ideal qumode possesses an infinte dimensional Hilbert space, allowing for high (in this case arbitarily high) density encoding of information in physical information carriers. This opens an alternative avenue for scaling QIPs, requiring dramatically less physical carriers compared to qubit-only systems. The inclusion of both CV and DV subsystems, termed the hybrid model~\cite{liu_hybrid_2026}, retains the convenient control and readout of discrete qubits while providing the larger state space and native interactions available to qumodes. 
For example, reference~\cite{brenner_factoring_2024} considers a factoring algorithm with the required physical resources independent of the number to be factored. Remarkably, this scheme requires only a single qubit and three oscillators, however requires prohibitivly large energy states of the oscillator.
Experimentally, we will not be able to realise arbitrarily high energy oscillator states, but it is still an open question of what information density is possible in accessible engineered quantum oscillator.\\
Hybrid spin-oscillator computation has been studied in the context of superconducting cavity-QED~\cite{hillmann_universal_2020, blais_circuit_2021, majer_coupling_2007, pan_continuous-variable_2023, eickbusch_fast_2022}, neutral atom in optical tweezers~\cite{kaufman_cooling_2012,lienhard_generation_2025, hwang_hybrid_2026}, and in the trapped-ion plaform~\cite{sutherland_universal_2021,gan_hybrid_2020,fluhmann_encoding_2019}. 
We shall focus on the trapped-ion implementation, where the DV system is the collection of qubits embedded in the electron energy levels, and the qumodes are the shared secular motional modes of the ion crystal within the trapping pseudopotential.\\
Trapped ions benefit from multiple native methods to drive, and couple qubit and qumode: 
The qumodes may be directly driven through parametric trap potential
modulation~\cite{heinzen_quantum-limited_1990, burd_quantum_2019,
burd_quantum_2021}, or through near mode-resonance RF fields; the qubit may be
driven either by optical fields (in the case of optical qubit encoding), or
through microwaves (in the case of ground state encoding) resonant with the
energy-state transition; 
the qubit and qumode may be coupled through qubit-state dependent potential shifts~\cite{drechsler_state-dependent_2020,leindecker_state-dependent_2026}, or through optical fields tuned near to motional-mode frequency sidebands of the qubit-carrier transition, for example through the Jaynes-Cummings interaction (\S~\ref{sec:Motional Coupling}), or the Mølmer-Sørensen (MS) SDF~\cite{sorensen_entanglement_2000}.
Taken together, these native control and coupling mechanisms, combined with the long coherence times of both the qubit and qumode, make the trapped-ion platform well-suited for exploring hybrid CV–DV quantum computation.\\
The apparatus developed in this thesis has demonstrated good qubit and radial
motional coherence times, provides access to multiple qubits and qumodes, and includes 
a single-addressing system for selectively driving their coupling. These capabilities make it 
well-placed for studying methods for scaling hybrid-system control.

    For any computing paradigm, we must establish a set of interactions required for universality. In DV-only systems, quantum universality may be realised by a finite set of Clifford operations plus one non-Clifford gate~\cite{nielsen_quantum_2010, aaronson_improved_2004}. CV-only universality can analogously be realised by a finite set of Gaussian operations plus one non-Gaussian gate~\cite{braunstein_quantum_2005,lloyd_quantum_1999}.
    The term Gaussian is understood in the position-momentum representation of the qumode, where the bare oscillator Hamiltonian is given by,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat H_\mathrm{osc,0} = \hbar\omega_\mathrm{osc}(\hat x^2 + \hat p^2),
    \end{equation}
    with quadratures,
    \begin{align}
        \hat x &= \frac{1}{2}(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger),\\
        \hat p &= \frac{-i}{2}(\hat a - \hat a^\dagger),
    \end{align}
    where $\hat a^\dagger,~\hat a$ are the creation and annihilation ladder operators of the mode with frequency, $\omega_\mathrm{osc}$.
    Gaussian operators are then the set of interactions containing at most quadratic terms in $\hat x$ and $\hat p$, therefore mapping Gaussian input states to Gaussian output states. \\
    The condition for universality of a hybrid CV-DV is somewhat ambiguous as both the CV and DV subsystems are themselves sufficient for universality, however this presents an opportunity: the freedom in selecting the chosen gate-set for universality, combining both Clifford, non-Clifford, Gaussian, non-Gaussian, and spin-oscillator interactions together, allows for selecting efficient, native, and simply accessed interactions of the physical platform. 
    We consider the over-complete instruction set presented in Table~\ref{tab:gateset}, consisting of spin-conditioned Gaussian and non-Gaussian interactions. This set of interactions on the bosonic subsystems are themselves universal, but the spin-conditioning provides a bridge between CV and DV subsystems, allowing for readout and control to be implemented on ancilla qubits.
    Specifically, we consider a cubic CV instruction set~\cite{lloyd_quantum_1999, eriksson_universal_2024, hillmann_universal_2020}, taking the Gaussian operations and supplementing with a non-Gaussian ``tri-squeezing'' gate, with Hamiltonian,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat H_T \propto \hat a^3 + \hat a^{\dagger 3}.
    \end{equation}
    Spin-conditioned tri-squeezing has been shown previously in our group~\cite{bazavan_squeezing_2024}, utilising the protocol given in reference~\cite{sutherland_universal_2021}, here termed the ``two-SDF'' method.\\
    The remaining non-linear Gaussian operations; squeezing, parity, and beam-splitter, may be generated through the same ``two-SDF'' mechanism, which we shall detail in the following section, and have also been previously demonstrated~\cite{bazavan_squeezing_2024,saner_real-time_2025,bazavan_easy_nodate,saner_quantum_2026}.\\
    The displacement operation can be realised either spin-independently by RF fields near resonance to the motional-mode frequencies (\S~\ref{sec:trap_charge}), or spin-conditioned using various SDF implementations, such as by the MS method using bichromatic fields (\S~\ref{sec:SDF}), or by static Stark-shift gradients due to an optical tweezer~\cite{leindecker_state-dependent_2026}.\\

    As highlighted in this section, the required unitaries for universal hybrid CV-DV control may all be accessed by a common protocol: The ``two-SDF'' method. This greatly simplifies experimental hardware and control complexity. 
    We shall give an overview of this method in the following section, and provide a practical route and demonstration utilising four optical frequencies with fixed relative detunings and phases.\\

    \subsubsection{Gate Implementation}
    \label{sec:two-sdf}
    To implement the unitaries given in table~\ref{tab:gateset} with spin-dependent control, 
    we use the ``two-SDF'' method proposed in reference~\cite{sutherland_universal_2021}. The non-linear bosonic interactions are effectively driven through applying two simultaneous linear spin-dependent forces with non-commuting spin conditioning, on a single qubit. 
    The two simultaneous SDFs, with Hamiltonians and parameters labelled by subscripts $\alpha$, and $\beta$, are applied off-resonantly relative to motional modes with creation operators $\hat a_\alpha^\dagger$ and $\hat a_\beta^\dagger$, respectively. The resulting Hamiltonian is given generally by,
    \begin{align}
    \label{eqn:two-sdf}
      \hat H_\mathrm{2SDF}(t)  &= \hat H_\alpha(t) + \hat H_\beta(t)\\
      &=
      \frac{\hbar \Omega_\alpha}{2}\hat\sigma_\alpha \left(\hat a_\alpha e^{i(\Delta_\alpha t + \phi_\alpha)} + \hat a_\alpha^\dagger e^{-i(\Delta _\alpha t + \phi_\alpha )}\right)\\
      &~+ 
      \frac{\hbar \Omega_{\beta}}{2}\hat\sigma_{\beta} \left(\hat a_\beta e^{i(\Delta_\beta t + \phi_\beta)} + \hat a_\beta^\dagger e^{-i(\Delta_\beta t + \phi_\beta)}\right),
    \end{align}
    where the individual SDF strengths are $\Omega_i$, the detuning from each mode is $\Delta_i$, the motional phase is $\phi_i$, and we ensure the spin-conditioning anticommute $\{\hat\sigma_\alpha, \hat\sigma_\beta\} = 0$, $[\hat\sigma_\alpha, \hat\sigma_\beta] = 2i\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\hat\sigma_\gamma$,
    where $\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}$ is the Levi-Civita symbols.
    The unitary dynamics of $\hat H_\mathrm{2SDF}$ may be evaluated by the Magnus expansion~\cite{magnus_exponential_1954}, with details given in references~\cite{sutherland_universal_2021,bazavan_easy_nodate,saner_quantum_2026}, and in appendix~\ref{app:magnus}. 
    To lend some intuition on the resulting physics, we highlight the main ideas here: The Magnus expansion expresses the generator of the unitary evolution as an infinite series of terms, with successive terms proportional to increasingly higher-order nested commutators of the Hamiltonian evaluated at different times. Due to the non-commuting spin conditioning, this series does not truncate, leading to arbitrarily high non-linear bosonic interactions effectively driven via the application of only two linear SDFs.\\
    To utilise specific non-linear terms within this infinite series, matching
    the desired interactions given in table~\ref{tab:gateset}, we constrain the
    relative detunings of the two SDFs to $\Delta_\beta = m \Delta_\alpha$,
    where $m$ is some integer given in the table. This immediately brings
    components of the Magnus terms matching the desired non-linear $\hat H_\mathrm{osc}$ into resonance. Any undesired higher-order terms that are also brought
    into resonance can be arbitrarily suppressed by increasing the detuning, as the effective coupling of
    the $n$th-order Magnus term scales as $\Omega_\mathrm{eff}\propto
    (\Omega_\alpha/\Delta)^{n+1}$ (relabelling $\Delta_\alpha=\Delta$ and assuming
    $\Omega_\alpha=\Omega_\beta$). The remaining error terms are far
    off-resonant, and may also be further suppressed by ensuring
    $\Delta \gg \Omega_\alpha\Omega_\beta$, and utilising appropriate smooth amplitude and phase envelopes for the pulses.\\
    In this regime, the unitary dynamics of $\hat H_\mathrm{2SDF}$ are well approximated by the evolution of time-independent effective Hamiltonians. For example, the effective squeezing interaction is driven when $m=-1$, and both SDFs act on the same mode, $\hat a_\alpha^\dagger=\hat a_\beta^\dagger=\hat a^\dagger$ yielding, 
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eqn:H_sqz}
        \hat H_\mathrm{eff,sqz} = \frac{\hbar\Omega_2}{2} \epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\hat\sigma_\gamma (\hat a^2e^{i\phi_p'} + \hat a^{\dagger 2}e^{-i\phi_p'}),
    \end{equation}
    where $\Omega_2=\frac{\Omega_\alpha\Omega_\beta}{\Delta}$ is an effective coupling strength,  and phase $\phi_p' = \phi_\alpha+\phi_\beta+\frac{\pi}{2}$. The derivation and forms of the other spin-conditioned effective non-linear bosonic interactions are given in appendix~\ref{app:magnus}. \\
    Frequency and spin-conditioning control of two SDFs therefore allow for the generation of the entire hybrid CV-DV gate-set proposed in table~\ref{tab:gateset}. 
    After a discussion on how we can characterise the oscillator state, we shall present the experimental scheme for generating these interactions, and demonstrate the creation of a squeezed state by this method.

    With the above methods we may generate interesting non-classical bosonic states, now we
    provide a method for characterising them.\\
    In contrast to a two-level system, the state of an oscillator may not be
    directly readout through state dependent fluorescence. This is due to the much larger state-space of
    the qumode, and the technical difficulty in direct motional readout on the ion-trap platform. To solve these issues, a modular readout scheme is used, involving indirect readout of the oscillator state via a discrete collection of controlled unitaries subject to an ancillary qubit, and computational basis measurements of that ancilla.\\
    The information is mapped to the ancilla by a Hadamard test~\cite{cleve_quantum_1998}, shown in
    figure~\ref{fig:tomography} (a). Here the expectation value of the operator $\hat O$ on the oscillator, $\langle \hat O\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat O\rho_\mathrm{osc})$, 
    is recovered through exclusive measurements of the ancilla qubit after qumode-dependent phase ``kick-backs'' are applied. 
    The Hadamard test has the convenient relation between computational basis measurements of the ancilla, and the applied operator, $\langle\hat\sigma_z\rangle = \mathrm{Re}\big[\mathrm{Tr}(\hat O\rho_\mathrm{osc})\big]$.\\
    Full state tomography may now be formulated by repeat measurements of the conditional displacement operation, 
    $\hat O = e^{-i\theta}\hat D(\beta)$, to recover the characteristic function, defined as $\chi =
    \mathrm{Tr}(D(\beta)\rho_\mathrm{osc})$, with $\beta$ being a complex parameter with displacement magnitude, $|\beta|$, and displacement axis, $\arg(\beta)$.
    Final computational state measurements of the Hadamard test circuit then yield,
    \begin{align}
    \langle \sigma_z \rangle &= \mathrm{Re}\big[e^{-i\theta}\mathrm{Tr}(D(\beta)\rho_\mathrm{osc})\big],\\
                             &= \cos(\theta)\mathrm{Re}[\chi] + \sin(\theta)\mathrm{Im}[\chi],
    \end{align}
    where $\theta$ may be set to $0$ or $\pi/2$ to recover the real or imaginary part of the characteristic function respectively.
    Each measurement provides only 1-bit of information on the (continuous) oscillator state, thus to approximate a useful reconstruction, 
    many samples with a range of $\beta$ values must be collected.\\
    Once the characteristic function has been measure, it can be converted to other familiar representations of the oscillator, such as in the Fock-state basis, or Wigner representation~\cite{wigner_quantum_1932, cahill_density_1969, vogel_determination_1989}. In this work, we shall keep to the conveniently measured characteristic function.\\
    To give two concrete example, the vacuum state characteristic function is the circularly symmetric Gaussian state,
    \begin{equation}
        \chi_\mathrm{0} = \mathrm{exp}\left(\frac{-|\beta|^2}{2}\right),
    \end{equation}
    while the squeezed state, generated by application of the Hamiltonian in equation~\ref{eqn:H_sqz} to the vacuum state, breaks this symmetry due to a contraction on one axis, and a corresponding expansion on the other~\cite{lo_spinmotion_2015},
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eqn:sqz_char}
        \chi_\mathrm{S} = \mathrm{exp}\left(\frac{-|\beta|^2}{2}\left(e^{2r}\cos^2(\phi)+e^{-2r}\sin^2(\phi)
        \right)\right),
    \end{equation}
    where the complex squeezing parameter is $\xi = re^{i\phi_s} = \Omega_2e^{i\phi_p'}t$, and 
    $\phi$ is the angle between squeezing axis and probe axis, $\phi'=\arg(\beta)-\frac{\phi_s}{2}$.\\
    Experimentally, $\hat\sigma_x$ conditioned SDFs are simply accessed by the bichromatic field method discussed in \S~\ref{sec:SDF}. The Hadamard test circuit is then decomposed to native gates, shown in
    figure~\ref{fig:tomography} (b), first described in
    reference~\cite{fluhmann_direct_2020}. The proceeding $I$ or
    $R_y(\pi/2)$ set the quadrature to measure; $\mathrm{Re}(\chi)$ or $\mathrm{Im}(\chi)$
    respectively.
    This circuit provides a practical method for characterising a single qumode,
    which we shall use in the following experimental demonstration. We note,
    similar to the constraints of state characterisation discussed in
    \S~\ref{sec:k-copy_char}, full tomographic reconstruction is
    intractable for multiple-qumodes due to the large state-space, even with
    reasonably truncated oscillator representations (e.g. imposing a Fock-state
    cut-off). In the case of characterising systems consisting of multiple
    qubits and qumodes, either reduced tomography~\cite{he_efficient_2024}, or simplified metrics of performance~\cite{valahu_benchmarking_2024} must be used.

\subsubsection{Two Spin Dependent Forces}
    The individual SDFs are generated by the same bichromatic field method
    described in \S~\ref{sec:SDF} --- i.e. simultaneous off-resonant blue-
    (BSB) and red-sidebands (RSB) of motional mode $\omega_\alpha$ are applied.
    To generate two simultaneously SDFs, we then require four optical fields with, frequency, phase, and
    amplitude control. The four required frequencies of the ``quadchromatic''
    field are shown schematically in figure~\ref{fig:2-SDF} with respect to the
    carrier frequency, $\omega_0$. Here, purple lines correspond to
    SDF$_\alpha$, and orange lines to SDF$_\beta$. In this example we show the
    SDFs acting on a shared motional mode, but in general they may act on
    different modes. Similar to the generation of the bichromatic field, we may produce this
    quadchromatic field through the application of four RF tones onto a
    single-pass (SP) AOM. These tones are generated using an arbitrary waveform
    generator (AWG)\footnote{\emph{Sinara Phaser} board}. We cannot control the
    absolute optical phase at the ion, however we can set relative phases
    between the four fields by applying phase offsets between the RF tones with
    respect to some fixed time point.
    As discussed in \S~\ref{sec:SDF}, the spin phase of the SDFs is set by
    the absolute phase of the RSB and BSB, $\hat\sigma_\phi =
    \cos(\phi_s)\hat\sigma_x + \sin(\phi_s)\hat\sigma_y$. To ensure we saturate
    the non-commutativity of the spin conditioning of the two SDFs, we require
    $\pi/2$ phase difference between the two spin phases,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eqn:spin_phase}
        \phi_{\alpha, s} - \phi_{\beta,s} = \frac{\phi_{\alpha,\mathrm{RSB}}+\phi_{\alpha,\mathrm{BSB}}}{2}-\frac{\phi_{\beta,\mathrm{RSB}}+\phi_{\beta,\mathrm{BSB}}}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}.
    \end{equation}
    In the following demonstration we set $\phi_{\alpha,\mathrm{RSB}}=
    \phi_{\alpha,\mathrm{BSB}}=\pi/2,\phi_{\beta,\mathrm{RSB}}=\phi_{\beta,\mathrm{BSB}}=0$,
    however we note that for composing multiple hybrid operations into a
    circuit, the resulting motional phase of the SDFs can lead to further
    constraints on the chosen RSB and BSB phases.\\
    The intensity of the quadchromatic field is ramped on and off with a rise
    envelope of $\sin^2(\pi t/(2t_\mathrm{ramp}))$ applied via the AWG, where
    $t_\mathrm{ramp}$ is the ramp duration. This ramp suppresses excess off-resonant
    excitations of the $A_1$ phase loop term given in the previous section (further details in appendix~\ref{app:magnus}), and
    so we must select ramp duration based on the characteristic scale,
    $t_\mathrm{ramp}\gtrsim\frac{1}{\Delta}$. \\
    We aim to balance the power in each of the 4 tones. This may be calibrated
    using an optical pick-off before addressing the ion, directed onto a fast
    photodiode, and measuring the beatnote contrast when applying any pair of
    tones. In the following squeezing demonstration, we balance the tones
    comprising the individual SDFs, but do not explicitly balance the power
    between SDFs, as the desired interaction scales as
    $\Omega_\alpha\Omega_\beta$, any imbalance leads to non-optimal interaction
    efficiency, but unlike imbalance of the RSB and BSB, does not increase
    coherent error terms.
    We observe that the duty cycle of the SP AOM can dramatically shift the
    power imbalance between all four tones, and so we take care ensuring that
    the total RMS power to drive one-, two-, and four-tones is the same as for
    the idle (no optical pulse) setting of the SP AOM, thus the only transient
    power drop is that of the ramp sequence. \\

    As the spin-state is prepared in an eigenstate of the spin-conditioned squeezing, we expect squeezing on the qumode, without any change to the qubit state. We use a total quadchromatic beam power of $53~\unit{\micro\watt}$, and set the base detuning, $\Delta=2\pi\times 50~\unit{\kilo\hertz}$. We suppress excess excitation of the off-resonant error terms by using a ramp of $t_\mathrm{ramp}=40~\unit{\micro\second}$.\\
    The state is characterised by the tomography method given in
    \S~\ref{sec:tomography}, with a discretisation set to collect a $35\times 35$ grid of displacement

    a total bichromatic beam power of $40~\unit{\micro\watt}$, and through fitting the 
    $\hat\sigma_z$ expectation for the time-dependent splitting of a vacuum state,

        \langle \hat\sigma_z \rangle = e^{-2|\beta|^2},~~~~~

    \end{equation}
    we measure an SDF splitting strength of $\Omega_\mathrm{split}=2\pi\times 7.2(4)~\unit{\kilo\hertz}$. \\

\subsection{Outlook}
    Hybrid systems offer access to large Hilbert spaces, even with modest ion numbers.
    In this section we outlined a convenient set of native interactions for universal hybrid qubit-qumode gates. We experimentally demonstrate the squeezing interaction generated via the ``two-SDF'' method of
    reference~\cite{sutherland_universal_2021}. This demonstration replicated previous work using the ``two-SDF'' method~\cite{bazavan_squeezing_2024}, however now shown on the newly built apparatus utilising a single-ion addressing system, and applied to the radial motional modes.\\ 
    Enabled by the single-addressing, and improved motional coherence times of
    the new system, we are particularly interested in exploring short hybrid
    circuits composed of multiple interactions acting on an array of qubits and
    qumodes. Specifically, we are interested in utilising the hybrid system with
    increased robustness against errors through the use of quantum error mitigation methods applicable to both 
    spin and oscillator subsystems. We outline one such method in the next section, with a preliminary demonstration on the spin subsystem.\\
    \subsubsection{Limitations}
    Using a single multi-ion chain to access multiple qubits and qumodes for
    scaling hybrid QC on the trapped-ion platform will be ultimately limited by
    spectral crowding of the motional modes, i.e. the average frequency spacing
    between neighbouring modes decreases as the number of accessible modes
    increases\cite{james_quantum_1998, schwerdt_scalable_2024}. \\
    The ``two-SDF'' method requires frequency selectivity to drive bosonic
    interactions exclusively on the chosen mode. 
    To selectively drive a single mode, the magnitude of the detuning $\Delta$ must follow $\Delta \ll \delta\omega$, where $\delta\omega$ is the frequency difference to the nearest neighbouring mode. As discussed in the ``two-SDF'' method section, the strength of each SDF must be less than the base detuning, $\Omega_\alpha \ll \Delta$, to well approximate the effective non-linear unitary dynamics. As a result of these two requirements, the
    mode-frequency splitting directly limits the attainable strength of the non-linear interaction in the adiabatic regime, resulting in increasing gate duration with increasing register size.\\
    A possible mitigation
    would be to rather scale the hybrid system using a collection of fixed-length
    chains with adequate mode-frequency splitting, and to transport quantum
    information between these chains either through ion transport whilst maintaining mode and spin coherence~\cite{walther_controlling_2012}, generating remote entanglement through photonic interconnects~\cite{luo_protocols_2009}, or through inducing collective motional interactions
    between separate ion-chains~\cite{valentini_demonstration_2025}. \\
    Another
    mitigation strategy could come from the pulse-shaping approach demonstrated
    for use in fast two-qubit entangling gates in the non-adiabatic
    regime~\cite{schafer_fast_2018}, i.e. when $\Delta > \delta\omega$.
    Introducing extra degrees of freedom in the pulse amplitude envelope could allow for
    driving the desired interaction on the selected modes, while returning all spectator modes to their initial state by the end of the pulse. However, we note that the accumulation of
    non-global geometric phases, and possible non-commutativity of interactions
    on shared qubit-oscillator states could prohibit the use of such a
    pulse-shaping scheme. Ultimately, the desired interaction, mode frequency spectra, and target gate duration will determine the dominant coherent errors. We leave such an analysis for future work. \\

\section{\textbf{Virtual Distillation}}
\label{sec:virtual_distillation}

With access to noisy, intermediate-scale quantum information processors, the
natural question is whether useful computations may be executed, without
requiring full fault-tolerant error correction codes. 
In this section, we explore a resource-efficient error mitigation technique, known
as virtual distillation, that can dramatically suppress the effect of errors on
the final measured outcome for expectation value problems.\\
This sections starts with outlining the goals of quantum error mitigation (QEM),
with the following section giving details on the virtual distillation method. We
then present an experimental demonstration suppressing errors on two-copies of a single qubit system. 
We finish this section with a discussion on future plans, and the extension of virtual distillation to hybrid
CV-DV systems.

\subsection{Error Mitigation}
\label{sec:QEM}
Noise coupled to a quantum system results in unintended coherent, and incoherent
evolution. Deviations of the realised state from the target state are known as
errors, and, if left unchecked, lead to the failure of quantum algorithms. 
This failure can either be due to a reduction of signal to noise ratio, or a
bias in the measured expectation value, resulting in misleading conclusions. 
Ultimately, the goal of quantum error correction (QEC) is to detect and correct
these errors through embedding information in larger Hilbert spaces~\cite{terhal_quantum_2015}.
Full QEC will allow for the execution of arbitrarily long algorithms ensuring errors (of
up to a certain distance) do not accumulate over time. However, current QEC
methods require large overheads in both qubit number and circuit depth, and are
therefore technically challenging on current quantum hardware.\\

Furthermore, QEC is typcially discussed with respect to
discrete ``gate-based'' quantum computers, which although promise
to be universal, pay for that privilege in often inefficient decompositions of
target unitaries into a discrete set of gates. 
This poses an opportunity for
near term devices to show quantum advantage using either short depth,
uncorrected circuits~\cite{preskill_quantum_2018}, or leveraging CV systems to access larger computational spaces~\cite{liu_hybrid_2026}. \\
However, the impact of errors on uncorrected computation must be carefully managed.
We consider algorithms that prepare some final state of interest and then take expectation-value measurements of some observable on that state.
Uncertainty of the approximated expectation value due to averaging over a finite
number of discrete samples, known as shot-noise, may be arbitrarily reduced by
increasing the samples, $N$. However, due to imperfect execution of a given
circuit, and coupling to the environment, there will be some systematic shift of
the estimated expectation value compared to the ideal value of the noiseless state, $\rho_\mathrm{id}$. This shift, or
bias, of the approximated observable may be reduced by the application of
quantum error
mitigation (QEM) techniques~\cite{temme_error_2017, cai_quantum_2023}. \\ 
QEM protocols are varied in nature, making it difficult to give an overarching mechanism of operation. Reference~\cite{cai_quantum_2023} defines QEM as
``... algorithmic schemes that reduce the noise-induced bias in the expectation
value by postprocessing outputs from an ensemble of circuit runs, using circuits
at the same noise level as the original unmitigated circuit or above.''
This highlights the main distinction to QEC protocols: the physical noisy state
is never corrected (often it is in fact degraded through the use of QEM) ---
rather the output from the noisy circuits is manipulated and postprocessed in
such a way that we \textit{effectively} recover an expectation value with
suppressed deviation from the ideal value.\\
\begin{figure}
    \begin{center}
    \noindent\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/pdf_figure/ch6/qem_schem.pdf
        }
    \end{center}
    \caption{
    A schematic explanation of the goal and cost of quantum error mitigation.
    Deviations of the measured expectation value of an operator $\hat O$ on a
    noisy state, $\rho$, and on the ideal state $\rho_\mathrm{id}$ may be
    separated into components originating from shot noise, and from an
    underlying bias. Shot noise sets the variance of the distribution of
    estimated expectation values, while the underlying bias shifts the center of
    the distribution. Error mitigation reduces bias at the cost of increasing
    variance. Here we demonstrate this by a (non-physical) oracle, filtering out
    all samples originating from measurements of the non-ideal state to
    reconstruct a bias-free estimate of the ideal expectation value.
        }
    \label{fig:qem}
\end{figure}
As a short example, consider a noisy state $\rho=\lambda\rho_\mathrm{id} +
(1-\lambda)\rho_\mathrm{err}$, where we have some probability of preparing the
ideal state, and some probability of preparing an error state,
$\rho_\mathrm{err}$. We take $N$ measurements of some observable on this mixed
state, collecting $n$ sample, $n=(n_0,n_1,...,n_N)$. Now consider the
(unphysical) perfect QEM, consisting of an oracle that ``knows'' if each sample
originated from a measurement of $\rho_\mathrm{id}$ or of $\rho_\mathrm{err}$,
and flags them as such. In a postprocessing step, we may discard all samples
associated with the error state, and retain the subset of samples of the ideal
state, $N_\mathrm{id}\subset N$. With this subset we may calculate the bias-free
expectation value, however at the cost of increased shot noise, as $N_\mathrm{id}\leq N$, and therefore
increased variance of the estimated observable under many such experimental
trials. This example, although fictitious, retains two important aspects of many
QEM methods. First, we effectively ``filter out'' undesired information by some
form of postprocessing, and second, this comes at a sample cost, where we
effectively exchange a suppression of bias to an increase in shot noise. This
trade-off is shown schematically in figure~\ref{fig:qem}, for this oracle QEM.\\

Error mitigation protocols are diverse in their resource efficiency, assumptions
on error-types, and scaling with system size. In this thesis, we consider the
virtual distillation
protocol~\cite{cotler_quantum_2019,koczor_exponential_2021,huggins_virtual_2021} ---
a QEM method, applicable to both DV and
CV systems, with few assumptions on the underlying noise
model~\cite{koczor_dominant_2021,foldager_can_2024}. It requires a fixed depth
circuit appended to the probed state-preparation circuit, and a reasonable
resource overhead of $k\times$ more physical qubits (or qumodes) for a
$k$-exponential decrease in error bias. With the apparatus constructed for this
thesis, we may begin to explore this exponential error suppression for small
system sizes. The following section gives
details on the mechanism and practical circuits.

\subsection{Virtual Distillation Protocol}
\label{sec:vir_dist_theory}

Virtual distillation is a purification-based QEM, utilising $k$-copies of a
circuit to reduce the final bias of the measured expectation value of a noisy
state, $\rho$. The term \textit{purification} is used to signify that the method
effectively approximates the pure state closest to $\rho$, with the underlying
assumption that the dominant eigenvector of $\rho$ approximates the ideal pure
state, $\rho_\mathrm{id}=\ket{\psi_\mathrm{id}}\bra{\psi_\mathrm{id}}$. 
The validity of this approximation is explored in detail in
reference~\cite{koczor_dominant_2021}, where they derive a bound for the
deviation between the ideal state and dominant eigenvector, termed the
``coherent mismatch'', $c$. This error provides the noise-floor for purification
based techniques. Favourably, for many error models and circuit types, $c$ is
negligible to relevant applications, and in fact scales inversely proportionally
to the number of qubits in a single copy, $N$, for fixed depth circuits.\\
The purified state may be accessed by,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:purification}
    \rho_\mathrm{pur}(k) = \frac{\rho^k}{\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^k]},
\end{equation}
where the denominator acts as a normalisation factor, and for $k=2$ is the usual definition for the purity of a mixed state. To elucidate the claim that this is the form of the purified state, we expand the spectrum of $\rho$, assuming that the dominant eigenvector is the ideal state, 
\begin{equation}
\rho = \lambda_\mathrm{id} \ket{\psi_\mathrm{id}}\bra{\psi_\mathrm{id}} + \sum_{i=1}^{2^N-1}\lambda_i\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i},
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_\mathrm{id} > \lambda_i$ for all $i$, and from normalisation, $\lambda_\mathrm{id}+\sum_{i=1}^{2^N-1}\lambda_i = 1$.
Inserting this expansion into equation~\ref{eqn:purification}, we find,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:rho_pur}
    \rho_\mathrm{pur}(k) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\left( \lambda_\mathrm{id}^k\ket{\psi_\mathrm{id}}\bra{\psi_\mathrm{id}} + \sum_{i=1}^{2^N-1}\lambda_i^k\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i} \right),
\end{equation}
with normalisation factor $\mathcal{N} = \lambda_\mathrm{id}^k  + \sum_{i=1}^{2^N-1}\lambda_i^k$. The exponentiation acts to relatively attenuate all error eigenvalues compared to $\lambda_\mathrm{id}$, with the limit in $k$ recovering the pure ideal state, $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\rho_\mathrm{pur}(k)=\ket{\psi_\mathrm{id}}\bra{\psi_\mathrm{id}}$.\\
The remaining question is how can we experimentally access $\rho_\mathrm{pur}(k)$? In references~\cite{koczor_exponential_2021,huggins_virtual_2021}, it is shown that the expectation value of the purified state with respect to the applied operator $\hat O$ is,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:pure_observable}
    \langle\hat{O}\rangle_\mathrm{pur}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{O}\rho_\mathrm{pur}(k)]=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{O}\rho^k]}{\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^k]},
\end{equation}
and crucially may be estimated without ever physically preparing $\rho_\mathrm{pur}(k)$.  
Instead the numerator and denominator may be found through preparing $k$-copies
of the noisy state, applying some derangement operation to entangle all copies
together, and measuring the desired observable on one of the copies. This can be
understood through the following identity\footnote{Huggins~\cite{huggins_virtual_2021} provides a proof of the identity, which we give an example for the case $k=2$ in appendix~\ref{app:derange}.} providing the numerator,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vd_identity}
    \mathrm{Tr}[\hat{O}\rho^k]=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{O}^{(i)}\hat S^{(k)}\rho^{\otimes k}],
\end{equation}
where $\hat S^{(k)}$ is the derangement operator, which permutes the $k$ copies of the state, and $\hat{O}^{(i)}$ is the operator acting on the $i$th copy. 
The denominator of the corrected expectation value is found by replacing the operator with the identity operator.
There is some freedom in the form of the derangement, here we shall give it explicitly as the cyclic permutation of $k$ objects,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:derange}
    \hat S^{(k)}\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\ket{\psi_2}\otimes...\otimes\ket{\psi_k}=\ket{\psi_2}\ket{\psi_3}\otimes...\otimes\ket{\psi_k}\otimes\ket{\psi_1}.
\end{equation}
Practically, if we can perform this multi-copy derangement, $\hat S^{(k)}$, and single-copy operator $\hat{O}^{(i)}$, on $k$-copies of the noisy state, we can postprocess a collection of circuit repetitions to recover the $k$th purified expectation value $\langle\hat O \rangle_\mathrm{pur}$.\\
In the following section we provide two possible approaches for implementing these unitaries and measurements, and give explicit circuit decompositions for both.
In~\S~\ref{sec:vd_exp} we explore the scheme experimentally, and particularly study the breakdown of error suppression due to correlated noise \emph{between} copies. We give details of the impact on suppression when the underlying independence assumption of the errors on each noisy copy is violated.

\subsubsection{Circuits}
\begin{figure}

        }
    \end{center}
    \caption{

        method using controlled derangement, $\hat S^{(k)}$, over $k$ copies,
        and controlled operator, $\hat O$, applied on one copy. The ancilla
        qubit is measured to extract $\mathrm{Tr}(\hat O\rho^k)$. Repeating the
        circuit without the controlled operator measures normalisation factor,
        $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^k)$, used to recover the virtually purified result,

        a $B$-circuit, here decomposed into two entangling gates and single

        }

We discuss two methods of implementing virtual distillation. The first being a general approach able to act on arbitrary Hilbert spaces using Hadamard tests, and the second being more resource efficient, however not simply generalisable to arbitrary sizes of each copy, and to multi-qubit observables.\\

We begin with the Hadamard test, to build further intuition on virtual distillation based on similar circuit topology discussed in~\S~\ref{sec:tomography} for CV-tomography.
The circuit for virtual distillation via an ancilla is shown in figure~\ref{fig:virdist_circuit} (a).
The derangement and desired operator, $\hat O$, are applied conditioned on
the ancilla qubit, with a final measurement only made on the ancilla. The
circuit is repeated for $N_\mathrm{shot}$ times and the outcomes are averaged to
recover the estimated real part of the numerator in equation~\ref{eqn:pure_observable}. The circuit is again repeated
$N_\mathrm{shot}$ times with the controlled operator replaced with the
identity operator. Subsequent averaging recovers the denominator, which may then
be used to recover $\mathrm{Re}\big[\langle \hat{O} \rangle_\mathrm{pur}\big]$.\\
The controlled derangement operation may be decomposed into a series of
controlled-swap gates~\cite{koczor_exponential_2021}, which for DV systems may
be decomposed to native single and two-qubit gates accessible on the ion trap
platform. For CV-systems, the derangement can be decomposed to a series of
Fredkin gates~\cite{fredkin_conservative_1982, gao_entanglement_2019}, which in
turn may be accessible by either
controlled-beam-splitters~\cite{gan_hybrid_2020,jeon_two-mode_2025} and/or
controlled-parity operations~\cite{choi_multimode_2026}. These interactions are available in our system by the ``two-SDF'' method, as highlighted in table~\ref{tab:gateset}.\\

The second method, known as the ``$B$-matrix''~\cite{huggins_virtual_2021}, is slightly more resource efficient, not requiring ancilla qubits, however does not simply scale for $k>2$, and for non-$\hat Z$ measurements. The approach recognises that for $k=2$ the symmetrised computational observable $\hat Z_2 = \frac{1}{2}(\hat Z\hat I + \hat I\hat Z)$ commutes with the SWAP operator, $\hat S^{(2)}$, and consequently so too do both observables required for virtual distillation,
\begin{equation}
    [\hat S^{(2)}, \hat Z_2 \hat S^{(2)}] = 0.
\end{equation}
Thus the two observables possess a common eigenbasis and may be simultaneously diagonalised by a single matrix\footnote{Note, we use a different convention than reference~\cite{huggins_virtual_2021}. This is done for a simpler decomposition to native gates on our apparatus.}, 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:bmatrix}
        \hat B =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        -1&0&0&0\\
        0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&0\\
        0&-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}&0\\
        0&0&0&-i\\
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
In the computational eigenbasis, the observable is now given by,
\begin{align}
    \langle \hat A \rangle &= \mathrm{Tr}[\hat B^\dagger\hat D\hat B\rho],\\
    &= \sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^2}d_x p(x),
\end{align}
where $\hat A$ is either $\hat Z_2$ or $\hat S^{(2)}\hat Z_2$, and the second line follows by the definition of the diagonalised operator $\hat D=\sum_xd_x\ket{x}\bra{x}$, and $p(x)=\bra{x}\hat B\rho \hat B^\dagger\ket{x}$ is the probability of observing computational state $\ket{x}$ after the basis change given by $\hat B$.\\
As a result, both numerator and denominator from equation~\ref{eqn:pure_observable} may be simultaneously measured by computational basis measurements after applying a two-qubit basis rotation\footnote{Although not discussed here, this method scales simply to $\hat Z$ measurements on copies containing multiple qubits through parallel pairwise rotations~\cite{huggins_virtual_2021}.}. 
The values are related to computational expectation values by,
\begin{align}
    \label{eqn:VD_ZZ}
    \mathrm{Tr}[\hat Z \rho^2] &= \frac{1}{2}(1-\langle \hat Z\otimes \hat I\rangle +\langle \hat I\otimes \hat Z\rangle +\langle \hat Z\otimes \hat Z\rangle  ), \\
    \mathrm{Tr}[\rho^2] &= \frac{1}{2}(\langle \hat Z\otimes \hat I\rangle + \langle \hat I\otimes \hat Z\rangle).
\end{align}
Figure~\ref{fig:virdist_circuit} (b) shows the $B$-matrix decomposition into native gates to our platform. A demonstration of this simple two-copy example of virtual distillation is given in the following section.

\subsubsection{Virtual Distillation on Two Copies}

We demonstrate virtual distillation via the $B$-circuit shown in figure~\ref{fig:virdist_circuit} (b) on two-copies of a single qubit. 
The qubit is encoded in the optical transition
$\ket{4S_{1/2},m_j=-\frac{1}{2}}\leftrightarrow\ket{3D_{5/2},m_j=-\frac{5}{2}}$ of a $^{40}\text{Ca}^+$ ion 
in a quantising field of $\sim5$~G. Gates are driven using 729-nm light focussed onto the ions by the
individual addressing system detailed in~\S~\ref{sec:Single Addressing
System}. Two-qubit entangling gates of the form $R_{xx}$ are implemented using
Walsh-1 MS gates on the in-phase upper radial mode, as characterised in~\S~\ref{sec:Two-Qubit Entangling Gates}. For this decomposition, we specifically require $R_{xx}(\frac{\pi}{4})$-gates. To calibrate these gates, two consecutive $R_{xx}(\frac{\pi}{4})$ pulses are applied, and a partial state-tomography protocol~\cite{sackett_experimental_2000} is utilised (as summarised by equation~\ref{eq:Fidelity}).\\

After calibration, the Walsh-1 $R_{xx}(\frac{\pi}{4})$ gate settings are the following:
$21~\unit{\micro\watt}$ total 729-nm beam power, $-15~\unit{\kilo\hertz}$ detuning from in-phase upper radial motional mode, $10~\unit{\micro\second}$ ramp duration (ramped on and off for both arms of the Walsh), and a $67.5~\unit{\micro\second}$ FWHM pulse duration for each arm. The total pulse duration including ramp durations is then $155~\unit{\micro\second}$.
Stark-shifts must be carefully calibrated to ensure consecutive MS gates have spin-phases set relative to the qubit. The Stark-shift for this power is calibrated using an SDF on a single ion (described in~\S~\ref{sec:SDF}), finding a shift of $2.49~\unit{\kilo\hertz}$. This phase is accounted for by updating the RF-tones on the SP AOM for subsequent pulses.
With these settings we measure a fidelity for two-consecutive $R_{xx}(\frac{\pi}{4})$ gates of $\mathcal{F}_2 = 96.0(8)\%$, with population component of $p_{01}+p_{10}=0.010(3)$, and parity contrast of $C=0.93(2)$. The inferred single $R_{xx}(\frac{\pi}{4})$  gate fidelity, assuming depolarising errors, is then $\mathcal{F} = 98.0(4)\%$. \\
Single qubit phase shifts, 
$R_z$, are executed virtually. As we require non-common $R_z$ phase shifts on the two qubits, we update the relative phases of the two
pairs of RF tones applied to the acousto-optic deflectors, used for generating the addressed spots. As these single-qubit gates are virtual, we expect negligible error contributions.\\
The final
computational-basis measurements are executed by state-dependent fluorescence,
described in~\S~\ref{sec:Stateprep}. 
From the single-shot outcomes, the joint probabilities $p_{ij}$, with $i,j\in\{0,1\}$ denoting the bright and dark states of ions 1 and 2 are recovered, respectively.
The required expectation values given in equations~\ref{eqn:VD_ZZ} are then simply,
\begin{align}
    \label{eqn:bmat_jointprobs}
\langle \hat Z\otimes \hat I\rangle
    &= p_{00}+p_{01}-p_{10}-p_{11},\\
\langle \hat I\otimes \hat Z\rangle
    &= p_{00}-p_{01}+p_{10}-p_{11},\\
\langle \hat Z\otimes \hat Z\rangle
    &= p_{00}-p_{01}-p_{10}+p_{11}.
\end{align}
To initially verify the performance of the physical implementation of the $B$-circuit, two heuristic tests are used\footnote{Process-tomography would be ideal for this two-qubit implementation, however we have not yet implemented this on our apparatus.}.
First, we want a clear indication that the transformation due to the applied $B$-circuit matches the convention given by the $B$-matrix in equation~\ref{eqn:bmatrix}.
Inspecting the
$B$-matrix, we see the central block acts
distinctly on the $\{\ket{01},\ket{10}\}$ subspace. As such, a method that preferentially probes this subspace would give a clear signal for conformity between the applied $B$-circuit and the desired $B$-matrix.
To access this subspace, a singly addressed $R_x(\pi)$-pulse is applied to prepare the initial state $\ket{01}$. A global $R_x(\theta)$ is then applied with variable rotation angle, $\theta$. Figure~\ref{fig:bmatrix_calib} (a) shows the measured two-qubit populations as a function of $\theta$ without the $B$-circuit applied. Panel (b) shows the populations with the global rotation and the $B$-circuit applied. The solid lines correspond to the expected populations inferred from equation~\ref{eqn:bmatrix}, while points are experimental data with $N=150$ samples per probed rotation angle. We can see good qualitative agreement between the observed data, and the desired behaviour suggesting the phase relationships enforced by the $B$-circuit indeed conform with the ideal $B$-matrix.\\
The second heuristic check verifies the conventions for recovering the numerator
and denominator in equations~\ref{eqn:VD_ZZ}, from the qubits measured
joint probabilities, given in equations~\ref{eqn:bmat_jointprobs}. 
To test this, a simple Ramsey experiment is performed on both
qubits simultaneously. The qubits are initially prepared in $\ket{00}$. 
A global pulse $R_x(\frac{\pi}{2})$ is then applied, followed by a final analysis pulse
$R_\phi(\frac{\pi}{2})$, applied with variable spin-phase, $\phi$. After these
pulses, the $B$-circuit is applied, and the two-qubit populations are measured. 
As the two qubits remain in identical pure-states to one another (up to errors in the parallel single-qubit
 gates), we expect the inferred purity to remain unity,
$\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^2]=1$. Given this, we expect the recovered numerator term to
be equivalent to typical Ramsey oscillations on a single qubit,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Tr}[\hat Z \rho^2] = \langle \hat Z \rangle_\phi = -\cos(\phi).
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:bmatrix_calib} (c) displays the post-processed purity and numerator terms extracted as a function of analysis pulse phase, $\phi$, with solid lines showing the expected behaviour of the ideal $B$-circuit. Again we see excellent conformity between experimental and desired behaviour, giving evidence that we are successfully applying virtual-distillation.\\
In the next section we use virtual distillation for its intended purpose --- to suppress the impact of errors. We will discuss the efficacy of virtual distillation under two different noise models, providing experimental evidence for both.

\subsubsection{Virtual Distillation under Correlated Errors}

To quantify the achieved suppression of errors through the use of virtual
distillation, we must characterise and inject errors with a known structure. As
such, applying randomised Clifford sequences, the same as those used in standard
randomised benchmarking (RB), are a convenient method for known error injection.
We frame the task of quantifying error suppression in the context of
single-qubit RB by comparing the exponential decay due to some
error-per-Clifford, $\varepsilon_\mathrm{raw}$, to the effective decay profile
of the error-mitigated observable, $\langle \hat Z\rangle_\mathrm{pur}$.\\

Figure~\ref{fig:bmatrix_injection} (a) shows the circuit implemented for
controlled error injection --- random Clifford sequences with a final inverting
gate are applied in parallel to both qubit copies, effectively driving
depolarising channels with total circuit error rate variable by sequence length, $m$.
The $B$-circuit is then applied followed by $\hat Z$ measurements.
Postprocessing of the two-qubit joint probabilities recover the error-suppressed
expectation value, $\langle \hat Z\rangle_\mathrm{pur}$.\\
The RB protocol is repeated without the applied $B$-circuit to recover the standard single-qubit
exponential decays, $\langle \hat Z\rangle_\mathrm{raw}$, and the corresponding
error-per-Clifford, $\varepsilon_\mathrm{raw}$.\\
Crucially, with this circuit we may explore two different error regimes: first,
we may apply independent single-qubit Clifford sequences to each copy, which
independently twirls the error channel acting on each qubit. This decorrelates errors between the
two copies~\cite{dankert_exact_2009,wallman_noise_2016} leading to favourable performance of virtual distillation.\\
In contrast, with the second regime we purposefully apply the same
Clifford sequence to each qubit, i.e. by enforcing $C_{i,L}=C_{i,R}$.
In this regime, any common-mode coherent errors acting on both qubits will not be
decorrelated by the parallel twirling (see \emph{k-copy} twirling in chapter~\ref{ch:k-copy}). These correlated errors can significantly degrade the performance of virtual distillation. We shall spend some time now to make this connection concrete.\\
Consider the $k$-copy mixed state, $\rho^{(k)}$, for a common-mode error channel,
\begin{equation}
    \rho^{(k)} = \sum_{i}\kappa_i \rho_i^{\otimes k}.
\end{equation}
Applying virtual distillation, using equation~\ref{eqn:pure_observable}, and the identity equation~\ref{eqn:vd_identity}, the effective measured observable is,
\begin{equation}
    \langle\hat{O}\rangle_\mathrm{pur,c}=\frac{\sum_{i}\kappa_i\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{O}\rho_i^k]}{\sum_i\kappa_i\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_i^k]},
\end{equation}
where crucially the trace operator is now within the sum.
Clearly, the components $\kappa$ are not exponentiated in the same manner as the $\lambda$ coefficients of the previously purified density matrix, $\rho_\mathrm{pur}$, in equation~\ref{eqn:rho_pur} --- as such the dominant state is no longer effectively amplified through derangement. In the specific case of common random unitary noise, discussed in~\S~\ref{sec:common_noise}, $\rho_i$ remains a pure state, $\rho_i = U_i\ket{\psi_\mathrm{id}}\bra{\psi_\mathrm{id}}U_i^\dagger$, as such the effective observable reduces to $\langle\hat O\rangle_\mathrm{pur,c}= \mathrm{Tr}[\hat O \rho] = \langle\hat O\rangle_\mathrm{raw}$, where $\langle\hat O\rangle_\mathrm{raw}$ is the expectation value of a single copy, $\rho=\sum_i\kappa\rho_i$. For this particular correlated error channel, virtual distillation is entirely ineffective.\\
Such correlated errors are commonplace in the ion-trap platform
due to noisy global control fields interacting with the ions. Under parallel
Clifford twirling, these global control field errors are simplified such that they can be quantified by a single value ``correlated error-per-Clifford'', $\varepsilon_\mathrm{corr}$, as discussed extensively in~\S~\ref{sec:common_noise}. As such, the relative proportions of uncorrelated, depolarising type errors, to correlated \emph{common noise} errors is required for predicting the performance of virtual distillation. The task of quantifying these errors is the subject of chapter~\ref{ch:k-copy}, and we shall use the developed \emph{k-copy} RB protocol here.\\
The analytic decay of the mitigated observable $\langle \hat Z\rangle_\mathrm{pur}$ when the two-copies are subject to a shared error channel, $\Lambda_T$, consisting of \emph{k-copy} twirled common random unitaries (parameterised by $\varepsilon_\mathrm{corr}$) and local depolarising errors on the two copies (parameterised by $\varepsilon_L$ and $\varepsilon_R$) is given by,
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:vd_correlated}
\langle \hat Z\rangle_\mathrm{pur} = (1-2\varepsilon_\mathrm{corr})^m\frac{\cosh\left(m\left(\varepsilon_L-\varepsilon_R\right)\right)}{\cosh\left(m\left(\varepsilon_L+\varepsilon_R\right)\right)},
\end{equation}
where we have assumed small errors.
This is simply calculated by extending equation~\ref{eqn:rb_meas} to include the action of the $B$-matrix, here using the superoperator form,
\begin{equation}
    p_{LR}=\langle\langle L,R|B\Lambda_T^m|00\rangle\rangle,
\end{equation}
where $p_{LR}$ is the probability of measuring bit string $LR\in\{0,1\}^2$. The effective observable $\langle \hat Z \rangle$ is then recovered by equations~\ref{eqn:bmat_jointprobs}. 
For small $m$, and assuming small errors, this may be expanded to second order as,
\begin{equation}
\langle \hat Z\rangle_\mathrm{pur} \approx 1-2m\varepsilon_\mathrm{corr} - 2m^2\varepsilon_L\varepsilon_R,
\end{equation}
where we can see that correlated errors maintain a linear decay, while depolarising errors are suppressed to a quadratic decay. We can also see that in the ideal case of exlusively depolarising type errors of equal magnitude, the effective observable decays as $\langle\hat Z\rangle_\mathrm{pur} = \mathrm{sech}(2m\varepsilon_L)$.\\

We demonstrate this behaviour experimentally by applying interleaved
single-qubit Clifford gate sequences on two qubits followed by the derangement
protocol given in the previous section. The interleaved sequences are
implemented by alternating the position of the single-ion addressing beam after
each Clifford, requiring a delay of $\approx 10~\unit{\micro\second}$ between
gates.
To ensure a baseline correlated
unitary error is present, the single-qubit gates comprising the Clifford
sequence are driven off-resonantly by $\delta = 2\pi\times 1~\unit{\kilo\hertz}$. 
For both identical and independent Clifford twirling, the correlated and
depolarising errors are quantified by \emph{k-copy} RB, with errors summarised
in table~\ref{tab:VD_k-copy}. The different twirling approaches change the
distribution of errors between correlating and depolarising, while retaining a
consistent total single-qubit error rate. It is interesting to note that the
independent twirling is not sufficient in this case to entirely decorrelate the
errors between the two qubits. These errors are used to predict the effective
error-mitigated decay profiles, and are plotted as a function of sequence
length, $m$, in figure~\ref{fig:bmatrix_injection} (b). The RB sequences are
repeated with the $B$-circuit applied, and the postprocessed $\langle \hat Z
\rangle_\mathrm{pur}$ are plotted. We can see that there is good agreement
between the expected mitigated decay profiles and the observed data. This
demonstration highlights the negative impact of correlated errors on the
performance of virtual distillation, as well as the importance of sufficiently
decorrelating errors, which we do here by local Clifford twirling.


\subsection{Outlook}

Quantum error mitigation is accessible to near-term quantum computers, under some circumstances allowing for resource-efficient, and substantial reductions in measurement bias. In this section we have provided some background on quantum error mitigation via virtual distillation, highlighting two possible implementations: a Hadamard test, and the $B$-circuit. We demonstrate the $B$-circuit on the trapped-ion platform, and use the protocol to explore the ability to suppress errors under unfavourable correlated error channels. These demonstrations provide the groundwork for two future research directions --- exploring the practical achievable advantage when extending to more than two copies; and extending to error-mitigation acting on a hybrid system.

\subsubsection{Extending to $>2$ copies}

    From equation~\ref{eqn:rho_pur}, we can see that increasing the copy number
    should exponentially approximate the dominant pure state. We want to observe
    this exponential behaviour, and so want to scale past two copies. To do
    this, we will use the ancilla-based Hadamard circuit, as shown in
    figure~\ref{fig:virdist_circuit} (a).\\
    For $n$ copies of noisy state, $\rho$, the controlled derangement can be
    decomposed to a minimum of $n-1$ controlled-SWAP
    gates~\cite{araki_space-time_2025}. Using accessible native gates, these
    controlled-SWAP are decomposed to $n_\mathrm{MS} = 11$
    $R_{xx}(\frac{\pi}{4})$ gates and at most $n_\mathrm{SQ} = 7$ single qubit
    $R_x(\frac{\pi}{2})$ rotations. Assuming that we can implement
    $R_{xx}(\frac{\pi}{4})$ with fidelity, $\mathcal{F} = 99\%$, and single qubit gates
    with fidelity, $\mathcal{F}=99.9\%$, we may calculate the expected total faults per
    circuit for a given copy size. Here, we assume the error on each gate is
    independent, and define expected fault number as $N_f =
    n_i(1-\mathcal{F}_i)$. Table~\ref{tab:VD_k-copy}
    summarises the gate scaling and expected fault number for the derangement
    operation. The first row gives the two-copy method via the $B$-circuit for
    reference.\\
    To effectively suppress errors, we (heuristically) desire that the
    derangement operation contributes less faults than the state-preparation of
    the noisy copy. Further, we desire that the total circuit has at most
    $N_f\approx 1$. From the table, we see that although it might be possible to
    demonstrate up to $k=5$ with our expected gate errors, this might be
    currently out of reach due to the large error contribution from the derangement. The difficulty in predicting the efficacy of the
    noisy derangement is that the impact of the errors on the performance of
    virtual distillation highly depends on their structure. Practically
    observing the suppression of circuit errors with copy number $k$, will thus
    provide evidence of the impact of individual gate errors on the derangement.
    It may also be useful to further characterise the individual gate errors
    using tomographic methods to predict the performance of the
    noisy-derangement.

\subsubsection{Virtual Distillation on Hybrid Systems}

    Hybrid systems, as discussed in~\S~\ref{sec:CVQC}, offer advantages
    in computation, due to the large accessible state space, with low numbers of
    physical information carriers. Quantum harmonic oscillators (qumodes) also have
    applications in quantum simulation~\cite{kang_seeking_2024} and sensing~\cite{degen_quantum_2017, valahu_quantum-enhanced_2025}. However, due
    to environmental and control noise, the performance and reliability of these
    systems has strong limitations. Virtual distillation offers a resource
    efficient method for suppressing errors in CV systems if a pure state is
    targeted. The proposal for virtual distillation originated from measuring
    an observable on a thermal qumode state effectively prepared at a lower
    temperature than the constituent noisy copies~\cite{cotler_quantum_2019}. \\
    We want to extend this protocol to a register of qubits and qumodes,
    applying transversal derangement, as seen in figure~\ref{fig:fredkin}, to
    suppress errors in hybrid systems. Particularly, the mitigation of dephasing
    and Gaussian errors on the qumode is an under-explored topic, and poses an
    opportunity for greatly reducing the bias in quantum simulation
    tasks~\cite{varona_towards_2024}.\\
    To access this, we require a Fredkin (controlled-SWAP) operation acting
    between an ancilla qubit and two qumodes. For certain Fock-state
    distributions, this may be realised by a single controlled-beamsplitter
    interaction~\cite{gan_hybrid_2020}, which we have simple access to via the
    ``two-SDF'' method in~\S~\ref{sec:two-sdf}. However, for the general
    case, we require both parity and beamsplitter operations, with control on
    either gate~\cite{filip_overlap_2002}. Demonstrating the Fredkin on a hybrid
    system is also of particular interest due to its reversible nature, and 
    the practical advantage that it conserves the total
    excitation number of the system~\cite{fredkin_conservative_1982}.\\

\subsubsection{Limitations} 

    Error mitigation is resource efficient, but only allows access to
    measurements of an \emph{effectively} error suppressed state,
    $\rho_\mathrm{pur}$ --- entropy is never physically removed from the
    system, as is the case for QEC. 
    The required samples for a fixed variance
    of the measured observable scales exponentially with the expected total
    number of faults in the circuit, $N_f$~\cite{cai_quantum_2023}.
    As such, QEM is only practically
    advantageous up to $N_f \approx 1$ (although this is somewhat a heuristic
    bound, and depends on many implementation details). QEM is thus suited for
    near-term applications on non-fault tolerant short depth circuits. \\

    As shown in~\S~\ref{sec:vd_exp}, correlated and structured errors between
    copies can greatly reduce the efficacy of virtual distillation. This may
    limit the practicality of virtual distillation on some experimental
    platforms relying on global addressing fields. This may be somewhat
    mitigated either through appropriate independent twirling of the
    copies or through random circuit compositions~\cite{wallman_noise_2016}. Furthermore,
    physically separating the copies using networked modules can ensure that
    errors originating from external environmental factors are no longer
    common-mode between copies~\cite{araki_space-time_2025}. \\
