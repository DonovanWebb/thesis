\subsection{The Qubit}

\todo{Quick rationalisation for choice of qubit}\\
    There is some freedom in choosing which of these 10 possible (with $\Delta m_j \leq 2$) quadrupole transitions we define as our qubit. Each transition has a varying coupling strength, here denoted by the relative Rabi frequency, $\Omega$, \todo{further define} depending on the polarisation and $k$-vector of the interacting light with respect to the B-field axis. The desired criteria for qubit and polarisation choice are as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
    \item Access to a closed-cycle transition for sideband cooling, (for details on this, see section~\ref{sec:Cooling}). Closed-cycle here means $\ket{4S_{1/2},~m_j = -1/2} \rightarrow \ket{3D_{5/2}} \rightarrow \ket{4P_{3/2},~m_j = -3/2} \rightarrow \ket{4S_{1/2},~m_j = -1/2}$.
    \item The qubit transition needs a large quadrupole matrix element, leading to a high Rabi frequency, $\Omega$, to enable laser-power-efficient, fast, gates. Polarisation, $\gamma$, may be optimised to increase this coupling.
    \item Ideally the qubit transition should have a low magnetic field sensitivity, to reduce the effect of magnetic field noise. Using a MuMetal enclosure around the experiment does relax this constraint, see section~\ref{sec:Magnetic Field}.
    \item To reduce the effects of off-resonant interactions due to nearby transitions, it is desirable to limit the number of accessible transitions near to the qubit transition. This is done by selecting a polarisation, $\gamma$, such that unwanted transitions are suppressed.
    \end{itemize}

    Given these criteria, in this thesis we use qubit transition $\ket{4S_{1/2},~m_j = -1/2} \leftrightarrow \ket{3D_{5/2},~m_j = -5/2}$ 
    define $\ket{\da}:=\ket{4S_{1/2},~m_j = -1/2}$, $\ket{\ua}:=\ket{3D_{5/2},~m_j = -5/2}$,



with time evolution defined by time-independent Hamiltonian,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_s = \frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2} \hat\sigma_z,
\end{equation}
where $\omega_0 = \frac{E_\ua - E_\da}{\hbar}$ is the qubit frequency.
Now we look at how to manipulate this spin state by coupling to external fields.


\subsection{Coherent Spin Control}

To manipulate the spin-state we consider periodic perturbations given by interaction Hamiltonian $\hat H_I(t)$, giving full time dependent Hamiltonian,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H(t) = \hat H_s + \hat H_I(t),
\end{equation}
Treating the ion as a two-level spin-like system, we consider the interaction between its
electronic state and a spatially varying electromagnetic field.

For a travelling optical field the transition-driving part of the interaction is generally given by,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_I = \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}(\hat\sigma_+ + \hat\sigma_-)\left(e^{i(\vec k\cdot \vec R - \omega t + \phi)} + e^{-i(\vec k\cdot \vec R - \omega t +\phi)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Omega$ is the Rabi frequency, $R$ is the ion position, $\omega$ is the optical frequency, and $\phi$ is some phase.
The Rabi frequency contains the specifics of the physical light-matter interaction, $\hbar\Omega\propto\bra{\ua}\hat H_I\ket{\da}$.
Assuming the phase variation of the field across the electrons spatial extent is small, we can expand the field around the ion position, $R$, for small relative electron position $r$, to generate a series
of electric and magnetic multipole interactions. For the qubit transition considered here ($S$ manifold to $D$ manifold) the electric dipole interaction is forbidden, as such in this thesis we exclusively consider the electric quadrupole interaction.\\
For plane travelling optical field given by,
\begin{equation}
    \vec E = E_0 \epsilon \cos(\vec k\cdot \vec R - \omega t + \phi),
\end{equation}
the quadrupole Rabi frequency is given by,
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\rm EQ} = \frac{1}{6\hbar}\sum_{i,j=\{x,y,z\}}\bra{\da}Q_{ij}\ket{\ua}\partial_i E_j\rvert_R
\end{equation}
where $Q_{ij}$ is the quadrupole tensor. For reference, for the simpler electric dipole, the Rabi frequency is given by
$\Omega_{\rm ED} = \frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_j\bra{\da}d_j\ket{\ua}E_j$, where $d_j$ is the dipole momement. We shall consider the general Rabi frequency, $\Omega$, for the rest of this section.\\

To evaluate the dynamics of $\hat H_I$ we go to a frame rotating at the qubit frequency, $\omega_0$, by going into the interaction picture with respect to $\hat H_s$: $\tilde H_I = \hat U_s^\dagger \hat H_I \hat U_s$ with $\hat U_s = e^{-i\hat H_s t/\hbar}$. Defining a relative detuning between the qubit and optical frequency as $\delta = \omega - \omega_0$, and neglecting fast oscillating terms with frequency $\omega + \omega_0$, the Hamiltonian is approximated by, 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:light_matter}
    \tilde H_I \approx \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\hat\sigma_+ e^{i(\vec k\cdot \vec R - \delta t + \phi)} + {\rm h.c.}~~.
\end{equation}
The dynamics due to the fast oscillating terms are safely neglected in the case of weak coupling $\Omega \ll \omega + \omega_0$.

For the rest of this section the ion is assumed to be stationary, thus the motional term contributes a simple phase factor, $\phi' = \phi + \vec k\cdot\vec R$. 

\subsubsection{Rabi Oscillations}
\label{sec:rabi_oscillations}

When the laser is tuned to the qubit resonance, $\delta = 0$, the Hamiltonian becomes time-independent, 
\begin{equation}
\tilde H_I = \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\hat\sigma_\phi,
\end{equation}
where $\hat\sigma_\phi = \cos(\phi)\hat\sigma_x -\sin(\phi)\hat\sigma_y$.
Exponentiating, this yields the unitary propagator,
\begin{equation}
    R_\phi(\theta) := \hat U(t, 0) = \begin{pmatrix}
        \cos(\theta/2) & -ie^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2) \\
        -ie^{-i\phi}\sin(\theta/2) & \cos(\theta/2) 
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
with $\theta = \Omega t$. Varying phase $\phi$ then sets the rotation axis in the $xy$-plane, with $R_x:=e^{-i\theta\hat\sigma_x/2}$ defined for $\phi=0$. 

Applying single qubit rotations to the initial state $\ket{\da}$ and observing the spin populations results in Rabi oscillations,
\begin{align}
P_\ua &= |\bra{\ua}R_x(\theta)\ket{\da}|^2\\
&= \sin^2\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right).
\end{align}
where $P_\da$ is the population observed in $\ket{\da}$.

\subsubsection{AC Stark Shifts}

When considering the optical interaction given in
equation~\ref{eqn:light_matter} for a large detuning $|\Delta| \gg \Omega$, the
Hamiltonian remains time dependent, and does not self-commute at different
times. The Magnus expansion is utilised to evaluate the dynamics. Details are
given in appendix~\ref{app:stark_shift}, with the resulting dynamics
approximating $\hat\sigma_z$ rotations of the form,
\begin{equation}
R_z := e^{-i\theta\hat\sigma_z/2}, ~~~~ \theta = \frac{\Omega^2t}{2\Delta}.
\end{equation}

In this thesis these $R_z$ rotations are often caused unintentionally due to
off-resonant coupling of other electronic transitions when addressing the qubit
transition. As such, for a given desired interaction and beam power, these Stark
shift rotations are calibrated and accounted for by updating the target rotation
axis of subsequent pulses.

From Earnshaw's theorem,
$\nabla^2 V = 0,$ a stable stationary point in 3D cannot be realised
using only static electric potentials, $V$, as if the potential is confining
in two dimensions, it will be anti-confining in the third. Therefore, to
achieve stable trapping, either an oscillating electric field (Paul
trap~\cite{paul1990electromagnetic}), or a static magnetic field (Penning trap) is
used.\\

Most general equation,
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(x,y,z,t) = \frac{V_{\rm DC}}{2d^2}(2z^2-x^2-y^2)+\frac{V_{\rm RF}}{2\rho^2}\cos(\Omega_{\rm RF}t)(x^2-y^2),
\end{equation}

equation of motion in $x$ is then 
\begin{equation}
    m\frac{d^2x}{d t^2} =  \left(\frac{eV_{\rm DC}}{d^2}-\frac{eV_{\rm RF}}{\rho^2}\cos(\Omega_{\rm RF}t)\right)x,
\end{equation}

This is of the form for 
Mathieu equation,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2x}{d \tau^2} + (a - 2q\cos(2x))x = 0,
\end{equation}
where we have defined $\tau = \frac{\Omega_{\rm RF}t}{2}$ $a=\frac{4eV_{\rm DC}}{m\Omega_{\rm RF}^2d^2}$, and $q=\frac{2eV_{\rm RF}}{m\Omega_{\rm RF}^2\rho^2}$ . 

gives stability, stable for $|a|,q^2\ll 1$.
In this case the ion dynamics are approximated by,
\begin{align}
    x(t) &\approx x_0 +  x_{\rm sec}(t)\left(1+\frac{q}{2}\cos(\Omega_{\rm RF}t)\right),\\
    x_{\rm sec} &= \cos(\omega_{x}t),
\end{align}
with secular mode frequency,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{x} = \frac{\Omega_{\rm RF}}{2}\sqrt{a + \frac{q^2}{2}}
\end{equation}
where we have secular and micromotion parts.
Assuming $q^2\gg a$, this simplifies To
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:pseudo_pot}
    \omega_{x} \approx \frac{eV_{\rm RF}}{\sqrt{2}m\Omega_{\rm RF}\rho^2}
\end{equation}

Similar form for $y(t)$, 
$z(t)$ is direct harmonic motion
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{z} = \sqrt{\frac{2eV_{\rm DC}}{md^2}}.
\end{equation}

Time averaging over the micromotion.
Can approximate this motion by pseudopotential with secular motion and micromotion.
Qumodes are the secular part, micromotion can be issue.
the resulting dynamics are well approximated as simple harmonic motion in 3D, corresponding to the potential,
\begin{equation}
    U_{\rm pseudo}(x,y,z) = \frac{m}{2}(\omega_{ x}^2 x^2 + \omega_{ y}^2 y^2 + \omega_{ z}^2 z^2),
\end{equation}

For $N$ ions, balance this confinement with Coulomb repulsion~\cite{james_quantum_1998}. Will get $3N$ common modes.

\subsection{The Qumode}
\todo{

The secular motion resulting from the pseudopotential is well approximated by the harmonic oscillator for small amplitudes. The quantum harmonic oscillator (qumode) is given by\todo{Check convention for ch6},
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm osc} = \frac{1}{2m}\hat p^2 + \frac{1}{2}m\omega_m^2\hat x^2,
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the ion mass, $\omega_m$ is the motional mode frequency, and $\hat x$, $\hat p$ are the position and momentum operators respectively. Expressed in terms of phonon creation and annihilation operators, $\hat a^\dagger$ and $\hat a$, 
\begin{align}
    \hat x &= x_0 (\hat a^\dagger + \hat a),\\
    \hat p &= p_0 (\hat a^\dagger - \hat a),
\end{align}
where $x_0=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_m}}$ is the ground state wave-packet extent, and $p_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar m \omega_m}{2}}$ is the ground state momenta.\\
The oscillator Hamiltonian is then given simply by,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm osc} = \hbar \omega_m \left(\hat n + \frac{1}{2}\right),
\end{equation}
where the number operator $\hat n = \hat a^\dagger \hat a$. This ``number'' or Fock-state representation suggested by this Hamiltonian labels eigenstates in the Fock basis as $\ket{n}=\hat a^{\dagger n} \ket{0}$. In the next section we describe coherent excitation of the qumode, and introduce the phase-space representation that naturally describe it.

\subsection{Driving Motion}

We now consider driving the qumode by a near-resonant spatially uniform electric field,
\begin{equation}
    E(t) = E_0  \cos(\omega t + \phi),
\end{equation}
with the Hamiltonian given by,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H(t) = \hat H_{\rm osc} + \hat H_I, ~~~~~~ \hat H_I = -q\hat xE(t),
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the ion charge. 
Going into the interaction picture with respect to $hat H_{\rm osc}$, we find,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde H_I = -\hbar \Omega\cos(\omega t + \phi) (\hat a^\dagger e^{i\omega_m t}+\hat ae^{-i\omega_m t}),
\end{equation}
where $\Omega = \frac{qx_0E_0}{\hbar}$. Expanding the oscillating field and assuming $\omega+ \omega_m \gg \Omega$, the fast oscillating terms are neglected giving,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:displace_H}
    \tilde H_I \approx \hat H_{\rm D} := -\frac{\hbar \Omega}{2}(\hat a^\dagger e^{-i(\delta t+\phi)}+\hat ae^{i(\delta t+\phi)}),
\end{equation}
where $\delta = \omega - \omega_m$.\\
In the case of resonant driving, $\delta = 0$, the Hamiltonian becomes time-independent, and we recover the propagator
\begin{align}
    \hat U &= e^{i\frac{\Omega t}{2}(\hat a^\dagger e^{-i\phi}+\hat a e^{i\phi})},\\
    &= e^{\beta \hat a^\dagger - \beta^*\hat a},
\end{align}
where the second expression is the conventional displacement operator,
$\hat D(\beta)$, where here $\beta = \frac{\Omega t}{2}ie^{-i\phi}$. The
displacement operator is used in the coherent state representation of the
oscillator, where the eigenstate of the annihilation operator, $\hat a$, is
given by $\ket{\beta} = \hat D(\beta)\ket{0}$. The action of the displacement
operator may be visualised in phase space, shown for resonance in
figure~\ref{fig:displacement}~(a).\\

When we consider the off-resonant case, $\delta \neq 0$, the Hamiltonian $\tilde H_I$ 
does not self-commute over time. The resulting dynamics are derived in
appendix~\S~\ref{app:geometric_phase} by expressing the interaction in the
Magnus expansion. Importantly, we recover two effects: first, we observe a
similar displacement of the initial motional state in phase space, but now
the trajectory traces a circle of radius $\frac{\Omega}{2|\delta|}$ which returns to the
initial motional state with periodicity $T_n = \frac{2\pi n}{|\delta|}$, for integer $n$. 
The displacement parameter $\beta$ is given by,
\begin{equation}
    \beta = \frac{\Omega}{\delta}\sin\left(\frac{\delta t}{2}\right)ie^{-i(\delta t/2 + \phi)}.
\end{equation}
Second, we see the build up of a geometric phase, originating from the second
order term in the Magnus expansion. The phase acts globally in this case, and
for a single closed loop is given by,
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(T_1) = \frac{\pi\Omega^2}{2\delta^2}.
\end{equation}

For arbitrary time, total propagator is then,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:displacement}
    \hat U(t) = \hat D(\beta(t))e^{-i\Phi(t)}.
\end{equation}

The phase space dynamics for the off-resonant displacement is shown in figure~\ref{fig:displacement}~(b).\\

From displacement in equation~\ref{eqn:displace_H}, what if we could add spin conditioning (e.g. $\hat\sigma_{\phi_s}$): get spin-dependent force, SDF,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm SDF} = \hat\sigma_{\phi_s}\otimes\hat H_{\rm D}
\end{equation}
This SDF is used in this thesis for three applications. First is applying to two-qubits simultaneously for MS gate, next is applying to one qubit on resonance for motional state tomography, finally is applying two off-resonant-SDFs on one qubit for non-linear bosonic interactions.

In this section we analyse how to generate an effective SDF using a combination of RSB and BSB fields.
Next section we discuss using geometric phase for two-qubit entangling gates.

The optical Mølmer-Sørensen (MS) scheme~\cite{sorensen2000entanglement} is used to generate the
SDF via a bichromatic laser field. Bichromatic refers to the simultaneous
application of two tones symmetrically detuned around the qubit carrier.
How does MS interaction use bichromatic field to get here?
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm bi} = \hat H_{\rm RSB} + \hat H_{\rm BSB},
\end{equation}
Label parameters with subscript $r$, $b$, for RSB and BSB respectively.
Set parameters to $\Omega_r = \Omega_b = \Omega$, $\delta_r = \delta_b = \delta_g$.
Expand Hamiltonian, and group together spin and motional terms, get,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm bi} = \frac{\hbar \Omega_{\rm SDF}}{2} \hat\sigma_{\phi_s}\left(\hat a^\dagger e^{-i(\delta_g t + \phi_m)} + \hat a e^{i(\delta_g t +\phi_m)}\right),
\end{equation}
where SDF strength $\Omega_{\rm SDF} = \eta\Omega$ spin phase $\phi_s = \frac{\phi_b + \phi_r + \pi}{2}$ and motional phase $\phi_m = \frac{\phi_r - \phi_b}{2}$, and we have used, $\hat\sigma_\phi = \cos(\phi)\hat\sigma_x - \sin(\phi)\hat\sigma_y$.
Comparing to equation~\ref{eqn:displace_H}, looks exactly like the desired displacement Hamiltonian now conditioned by $\sigma_{\phi_s}$.

    We perform two-qubit entangling gates using the Mølmer-Sørensen (MS)
    interaction~\cite{sorensen2000entanglement}. This interaction is the same described SDF from the
    previous section, but applied globally to two ions. The MS interaction
    relies on the spin dependent geometric phase accumulated during the motional
    displacement~\cite{ozeri2011tutorial}. To create a two-qubit entangled state, a differential
    geometric phase of $\pi/2$ must be accumulated between the two-qubit basis
    states~\cite{ozeri2011tutorial}. To ensure there is no residual motional entanglement, the final
    motional state must return to the initial state. In practise, using an SDF
    detuned by $\delta_g$, this is achieved by applying the MS interaction for a
    duration $t_g = 2n\pi/\delta_g$, where $n$ is the number of ``loops''. The MS gate is a universal two-qubit gate, and
    along with only single qubit gates, constitutes a universal gate set for
    discrete quantum algorithms.\\  

Set spin phase, $\phi_s = 0$, so we have $\hat \sigma_x$ conditioned displacement.
If applied to two qubits, replace $\hat\sigma_x$ with $\hat S_{xx}=\hat\sigma_x\otimes \hat I + \hat I\otimes\hat\sigma_x$, and set $\phi_m = 0$
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm MS} = \frac{\hbar \Omega_{\rm SDF}}{2}\hat S_{xx}(\hat a^\dagger e^{-i\delta_g t}+\hat a e^{i\delta_g t})
\end{equation}

Taking Magnus expansion, find same form as for motional displacement and geometric phase,
\begin{equation}
    \hat U_{\rm MS}(t) = \hat D(\beta(t))e^{-i\Phi(t)}.
\end{equation}
but now both $\beta$ and $\phi$ have spin conditioning,
\begin{equation}
    \beta(t) = \frac{\Omega_{\rm SDF}}{\delta_g}\sin\left(\frac{\delta_g t}{2}\right)ie^{-i(\delta_g t/2)}\hat S_{xx},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(t) = \frac{\Omega_{\rm SDF}^2}{4\delta_g}\left(t-\frac{\sin(\delta_g t)}{\delta_g}\right)\hat S_{xx}^2.
\end{equation}
For loop closure time $T_n = \frac{2\pi n}{|\delta|}$, results in time-independent effective Hamiltonian, with no motional component, with propagator defined as,
\begin{equation}
    R_{xx}(\theta) := e^{-i\theta\hat\sigma_x\otimes\hat\sigma_x/2}
\end{equation}
where we have neglected any global phases,
\begin{equation}
\theta = \frac{\Omega_{\rm SDF}^2T_n}{\delta_g}.
\end{equation}

To maximally entangle two initially separable spins want $\theta=\pi/2$ so we get,
\begin{align}
\delta_g &= 2\sqrt{n}\Omega_{\rm SDF},\\
T_n &= \frac{\pi\sqrt{n}}{\Omega_{\rm SDF}}.
\end{align}
