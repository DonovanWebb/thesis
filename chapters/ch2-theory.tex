This thesis considers control over a hybrid spin-motion system, as shown
schematically in Figure~\ref{fig:spin_motion}. For an $N$-ion chain, we have access to $N$-qubits, defined by
electronic transitions of the ions, and $3N$-qumodes (quantum-harmonic oscillators), emerging from the collective motion
of the ion chain.

In this chapter, we lay out the foundational concepts and interactions that will
be used throughout this thesis. We begin by considering the choice of ion, \ca, and
its relevant optical transitions. We then abstract the electronic levels of the
ion to a single two-level system, the qubit, and discuss coherent control using
oscillating electric fields. Next we consider the motion of the ions, how this
motion originates using a linear Paul trap, and how to coherently excite it.
This chapter ends with a discussion of the interactions utilised for hybrid
spin-motion control, with an emphasis on this control for realising two-qubit
entangling gates via the shared motional modes.

\section{The Ion}
\label{sec:Transitions}


    There are many choices of ion for trapped-ion quantum computing~\cite{bruzewicz_trapped-ion_2019}.
    Selecting a species is a compromise between capability and experimental
    ease-of-use. For example, ions with hyperfine structure, compared to those
    without, have many more energy levels with accessible clock-transitions that
    are insensitive to external magnetic field fluctuations~\cite{langer_long-lived_2005,harty_high-fidelity_2014}. This
    insensitivity is of great importance when envisioning scaled
    quantum-computing with long coherence times enabling deep circuits. However,
    practically the extra hyperfine splitting adds experimental complexity,
    requiring more control for state-preparation, and more channels for leakage
    errors. 
    Thus, to ease the control requirements, a species without hyperfine structure is selected, allowing the focus of the constructed apparatus to be directed instead towards the planned demonstrations of hybrid control of a multi-ion chain.
    The choice of species is further restricted by the desire to use
    visible wavelengths for the light-matter interactions, and an optical
    qubit-transition with reasonable excited state lifetimes. We require the
    wavelength associated with the qubit frequency to have commercially
    available narrow-linewidth lasers with power outputs of $\approx
    5~\unit{\watt}$. Given those conditions, we selected \ca, with a qubit defined by the
    optical transition at 729-nm, between the ground manifold $\ket{4S_{1/2}}$
    and the excited manifold $\ket{3D_{5/2}}$. 
    This optical transition is electric-dipole
    forbidden due to the symmetries of the $\ket{4S_{1/2}}$ and $\ket{3D_{5/2}}$
    electronic wavefunctions. Consequently, the excited $\ket{3D_{5/2}}$ manifold is 
    metastable, with a natural
    lifetime of $\tau \approx 1.1~\unit{\second}$~\cite{barton_measurement_2000}. 
    We shall refer to the transition between the two manifolds as either the
    quadrupole transition, or the 729-nm transition.
    The energy levels for
    \ca are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ion}, 
    with the laser wavelengths used to drive selected transitions highlighted.

    The 397-nm and 866-nm lasers are used to form a dissipative closed-cycle
    transition useful for both state-dependent fluorescence
    (\S~\ref{sec:Measurement}), and for laser cooling (\S~\ref{sec:Cooling}).
    The 854-nm laser is used to deshelve the electron from the $\ket{3D_{5/2}}$
    manifold for qubit state preparation (\S~\ref{sec:Stateprep}). Details on
    these lasers can be found in~\S~\ref{sec:Laser systems}.

    An external magnetic field of $\approx5~\unit{\gauss}$ provides the
    quantisation axis for the ion.  This field enables both polarisation and
    frequency selectivity of the atomic transitions, ensuring unintended
    off-resonant excitations are suppressed when optically driving interactions. The
    Zeeman splitting magnitudes are highlighted for the ground and excited
    manifold used for the qubit. The transition frequencies thus have a dependence on field strength given by the difference in magnetic moments of the two states. For the quadrupole transition this is given by,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eqn:mag_sens}
        \left| \frac{df}{dB}\right| = \Delta \mu_B/h, ~~~~~~~~ \Delta = g_Dm_{D}- g_Sm_{S},
    \end{equation}
    where $\mu_B$ is the Bohr magneton, and $g_D$ and $g_S$ are the $g$-factors for the manifolds $\ket{3D_{5/2}}$ and $\ket{4S_{1/2}}$ respectively. The magnetic field sensitivities are then,
    $\lvert \frac{df}{dB}\rvert = \{-2.799, -1.119,
    0.561, 2.241, 3.922\}~\unit{\MHz\per\gauss}$ between ground state $\ket{\da}$ and excited states with varying $m_j$, $\ket{3D_{5/2},~m_j = \{-5/2, -3/2, -1/2, 1/2, 3/2\}}$ respectively.

    The quantising magnetic field is set perpendicular to the 729-nm laser beam access. As such, the coupling strength of the various quadrupole transitions for a linear-polarised optical field is set exclusively by the angle, $\gamma$, between the magnetic field axis and the optical polarisation axis. This relationship is plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:quadrupole}.
    Figure~\ref{fig:quadrupole}~(a), shows the relative coupling strength
    (here given as Rabi frequency, which is discussed in~\S~\ref{sec:qubit_control}) of each transition as the angle $\gamma$ is varied.
    To maximise the coupling of one transition, and suppress other possible transitions, the polarisation of the 729-nm laser can be set to $\gamma = \frac{\pi}{2}$. This would be a good choice if high-strength coupling interactions are desired to minimise off-resonant driving of spectator interactions. If it is desirable to access multiple quadrupole transitions, setting $\gamma = \frac{\pi}{4}$ is a convenient choice.
    At the magnetic field strength of $\approx 5~\unit{\gauss}$, the relative frequency splitting and strengths of the interactions are shown for $\gamma = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\gamma=\frac{\pi}{4}$ in panels (b) and (c) respectively.



\section{Spin}
\subsection{The Qubit}

The particular choice of qubit, highlighted in Figure~\ref{fig:ion},
$\ket{\da}:=\ket{4S_{1/2},~m_j = -1/2} \leftrightarrow \ket{\ua}:=\ket{3D_{5/2},~m_j = -5/2}$, is convenient as the 729-nm polarisation when set to $\gamma=\frac{\pi}{2}$ can maximise its coupling. Furthermore, due to the dipole selection rule, $|\Delta m_j| \leq 1$,
a closed-cycle transition for sideband cooling (details in \S~\ref{sec:Cooling}) is available using this excited $\ket{3D_{5/2}}$ state. 

The computational eigenstates of the qubit, $\ket{\da}$, $\ket{\ua}$, have eigenvalues,
\begin{equation}
    \hat \sigma_z \ket{\da} = -\ket{\da},~~~~~\hat \sigma_z \ket{\ua} = +\ket{\ua},
\end{equation}
and the dynamics of the system are giving by the spin Hamiltonian,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_s = \frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2} \hat\sigma_z,
\end{equation}
where $\omega_0$ is the qubit transition frequency.

We define transition elements $\hat\sigma_+ = \ket{\ua}\bra{\da}$, and $\hat\sigma_- = \ket{\da}\bra{\ua}$.
The next section outlines how to physically realise such transitions.


\subsection{Coherent Spin Control}
\label{sec:qubit_control}

To manipulate the spin-state we consider periodic perturbations given by the interaction Hamiltonian $\hat H_I(t)$, with the full time dependent Hamiltonian of the system given by,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H(t) = \hat H_s + \hat H_I(t).
\end{equation}
The perturbation is treated semi-classically, consisting of the interaction between the
quantised electronic states of the ion and a classical spatially varying electromagnetic field.

For a travelling monochromatic optical field (almost always from a laser) the transition-driving part of the interaction is generally given by,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_I = \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}(\hat\sigma_+ + \hat\sigma_-)\left(e^{i(\vec k\cdot \vec R - \omega t + \phi)} + e^{-i(\vec k\cdot \vec R - \omega t +\phi)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Omega$ is the Rabi frequency, $\vec k$ is the wave vector, $\vec R$ is the ion position, $\omega$ is the optical frequency, and $\phi$ is the optical phase.
The Rabi frequency contains the specifics of the physical light-matter interaction, $\hbar\Omega\propto\bra{\ua}\hat H_I\ket{\da}$.
Assuming the phase variation of the optical field across the electrons spatial extent is small, the perturbation can be expanded around the ion position, $R$, for small relative electron position, to generate a series
of electric and magnetic multipole interactions. For the chosen optical qubit transition the electric dipole interaction is forbidden. Therefore, in this thesis we exclusively consider the electric quadrupole interaction.

For a plane travelling optical field given by,
\begin{equation}
    \vec E = E_0 \vec\epsilon \cos(\vec k\cdot \vec R - \omega t + \phi),
\end{equation}
where $\vec\epsilon$ is the polarisation vector, and the quadrupole Rabi frequency is given by,
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\rm EQ} = \frac{1}{6\hbar}\sum_{i,j=\{x,y,z\}}\bra{\da}Q_{ij}\ket{\ua}\partial_i E_j\rvert_R
\end{equation}
where $Q_{ij}$ is the quadrupole tensor~\cite{james_quantum_1998}\footnote{For reference, the simpler electric dipole interaction has Rabi frequency is given by
$\Omega_{\rm ED} = \frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_j\bra{\da}d_j\ket{\ua}E_j$, where $d_j$ is the dipole moment}. Apart from the dependence on polarisation and $k$-vector discussed in Figure~\ref{fig:quadrupole}, the details of the quadrupole derived Rabi-frequency are not relevant for the remainder of this section, and a general Rabi frequency, denoted by $\Omega$, is instead considered.

To evaluate the dynamics of $\hat H_I$ we go to a frame rotating at the qubit frequency, $\omega_0$. This interaction picture with respect to $\hat H_s$ is $\tilde H_I = \hat U_s^\dagger \hat H_I \hat U_s$ with $\hat U_s = e^{-i\hat H_s t/\hbar}$. 
Defining the detuning between the qubit and optical frequencies as $\delta = \omega-\omega_0$, and neglecting the rapidly oscillating terms at frequency $\omega+\omega_0$ under the rotating-wave approximation, the Hamiltonian is approximated as,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:light_matter}
    \tilde H_I \approx \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\hat\sigma_+ e^{i(\vec k\cdot \vec R - \delta t + \phi)} + {\rm h.c.}~~.
\end{equation}
The dynamics due to the fast oscillating terms are safely neglected in the case of weak coupling $\Omega \ll \omega + \omega_0$.

For the rest of this subsection the ion is assumed to be stationary over time, thus the motional term contributes a simple phase factor which can be absorbed into a global phase, $\phi' = \phi + \vec k\cdot\vec R$. 

\subsubsection{Rabi Oscillations}
\label{sec:rabi_oscillations}

When the optical field frequency is tuned to the qubit resonance, $\delta = 0$, the Hamiltonian becomes time-independent, 
\begin{equation}
\tilde H_I = \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\hat\sigma_\phi,
\end{equation}
where $\hat\sigma_\phi = \cos(\phi)\hat\sigma_x -\sin(\phi)\hat\sigma_y$.
Exponentiating\footnote{Using identity $e^{-i\theta \vec{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}/2}
=\hat I \cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)-i(\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma})\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$, where $\vec n$ is rotation-axis unit vector, and $\vec\sigma$ is the Pauli vector.}, this yields the unitary propagator,
\begin{equation}
    \hat R_\phi(\theta) := \hat U(t, 0) = \begin{pmatrix}
        \cos(\theta/2) & -ie^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2) \\
        -ie^{-i\phi}\sin(\theta/2) & \cos(\theta/2) 
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
which is a rotation on the Bloch sphere by angle $\theta = \Omega t$, around the Bloch vector, $\hat\sigma_\phi$. Varying phase $\phi$ then sets the rotation axis in the $xy$-plane, with $\hat R_x(\theta):=e^{-i\theta\hat\sigma_x/2}$ defined for $\phi=0$. 
Applying single qubit rotations to the initial state $\ket{\da}$ and observing the spin populations results in spin-state transfers, termed Rabi oscillations,
\begin{align}
P_\ua &= |\bra{\ua}\hat R_x(\Omega t)\ket{\da}|^2\\
&= \sin^2\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right).
\end{align}
where $P_\ua$ is the population observed in $\ket{\ua}$.

\subsubsection{AC Stark Shifts}
\label{sec:ac_stark}

When considering the optical interaction given in
equation~\ref{eqn:light_matter} for a large detuning $|\Delta| \gg \Omega$, the
Hamiltonian remains time dependent, and does not self-commute at different
times. The Magnus expansion is utilised to evaluate the dynamics. Details of the expansion are given in Appendix~\ref{app:magnus}. The unitary dynamics are well approximated by $\hat\sigma_z$ rotations of the form,
\begin{equation}
\hat R_z := e^{-i\theta\hat\sigma_z/2}, ~~~~ \theta = \frac{\Omega^2t}{2\Delta}.
\end{equation}
In this thesis, these $\hat R_z$ rotations are often caused unintentionally due to
off-resonant coupling of other electronic transitions when addressing the qubit
transition. As such, for a given desired interaction and beam power, these Stark
shift rotations are calibrated and accounted for by updating the target rotation
axis of subsequent pulses.\\

Having established spin-only control of a single qubit, we now consider motional control as a prerequisite for two-qubit entanglement. For trapped-ion qubits encoded in low-lying electronic states, the direct Coulomb interaction is effectively independent of the internal qubit state, motivating the use of shared motion and state-dependent forces to mediate entanglement. 


\section{Motion}
The potential used to trap the ions also dictates their motion. 
Here, we describe the motion of a single trapped ion in a radio-frequency (RF) Paul trap, before introducing the resulting qumodes.

\subsection{Linear Paul Trap}
\label{sec:trap_charge}

From Earnshaw's theorem,
$\nabla^2 V = 0,$ a stable stationary point in 3D cannot be realised
using only static electric potentials. If the potential is confining
in two dimensions it must be anti-confining in the third. Therefore, to
achieve stable trapping, either a supplementary oscillating electric field, as used in RF Paul
traps~\cite{paul_electromagnetic_1990}, or a static magnetic field, as used in Penning
traps~\cite{dehmelt_experiments_1990}, is required.\\

In this thesis, we use an RF Paul trap (hardware details in~\S~\ref{sec:The Ion
Trap}). Radial confinement is provided by an oscillating radial electric
potential, while the axial confinement is provided by a static axial potential.
Both potentials are considered to be ideal quadrupolar electric potential. The combined potential is given as,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:potential}
    \Phi(x,y,z,t) = \frac{V_{\rm DC}}{2d^2}(2z^2-x^2-y^2)+\frac{V_{\rm RF}}{2\hat\rho^2}\cos(\Omega_{\rm RF}t)(x^2-y^2),
\end{equation}
where $V_{\rm DC}$ is the voltage applied to axial ``endcap'' electrodes which are distance $d$ from the trapping point. $V_{\rm RF}$ is the RF voltage with frequency $\Omega_{\rm RF}$ applied to the radial RF electrodes which are distance $\hat\rho$ from the trapping point. This potential considers a highly symmetric field configuration with the axial potential confining in the $z$ direction while equally anti-confining in both radial directions.
The radial potential has no axial component and periodically alternates between confinement in $x$ with anti-confinement in $y$, and confinement in $y$ with anti-confinement in $x$.

From this potential the equation of motion in the radial $x$ for a trapped ion with charge $+e$ is,
\begin{equation}
    m\frac{d^2x}{d t^2} =  \left(\frac{eV_{\rm DC}}{d^2}-\frac{eV_{\rm RF}}{\hat\rho^2}\cos(\Omega_{\rm RF}t)\right)x,
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the mass of the ion. This motion is thus described by the Mathieu equation,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2x}{d \tau^2} + (a - 2q\cos(2\tau))x = 0,
\end{equation}
where we have defined $\tau = \frac{\Omega_{\rm RF}t}{2}$, $a=-\frac{4eV_{\rm DC}}{m\Omega_{\rm RF}^2d^2}$, and $q=-\frac{2eV_{\rm RF}}{m\Omega_{\rm RF}^2\hat\rho^2}$. 
Analysis of the two parameters, $a$, $q$, of the Mathieu equation yield regions of stability (motional amplitude converges) and instability (motional amplitude diverges). For the ``lowest stability region'', the regime relevant to our trap, the motion is stable when $|a|,q^2\ll 1$~\cite{leibfried_quantum_2003}.
In this regime, the ion dynamics are well approximated by the motion,
\begin{equation}
    x(t) \approx x_{\rm eq} +  x_{\rm sec}(t)\left(1+\frac{q}{2}\cos(\Omega_{\rm RF}t)\right),
\end{equation}
where $x_{\rm eq}$ is the equilibrium position, and $x_{\rm sec}$ is a secular motional term,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:secular}
    x_{\rm sec}(t) = A\cos(\omega_{x}t+\theta_0),
\end{equation}
with phase, $\theta_0$ and amplitude, $A$, set by initial conditions and a frequency, 
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{x} = \frac{\Omega_{\rm RF}}{2}\sqrt{a + \frac{q^2}{2}}.
\end{equation}
A similar form exists for motion in $y$. The non-secular component of the motion is termed ``micromotion'', and provides a fast oscillation superimposed on the secular motion. 
Due to the different time-scales of the two motional terms, and the relatively smaller amplitude of the micromotion term, averaging over periods greater than $\frac{1}{\Omega_{\rm RF}}$ often is sufficient to negate the effects of the fast oscillations. In this case, the motion is well described by simple harmonic motion in 3D with a ``pseudopotential'' given by,
\begin{equation}
    U_{\rm pseudo}(x,y,z) = \frac{m}{2}(\omega_{ x}^2 x^2 + \omega_{ y}^2 y^2 + \omega_{ z}^2 z^2).
\end{equation}
The axial mode is given exclusively by the DC term,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{z} = \sqrt{\frac{2eV_{\rm DC}}{md^2}}.
\end{equation}
The radial terms are given by,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:pseudo_pot}
    \omega_{x} = \omega_{y}  = \sqrt{\omega_r^2 - \omega_z^2},
\end{equation}
where $\omega_r$ is the radial trapping frequency in the limit of $q^2\gg a$, 
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{r} = \frac{eV_{\rm RF}}{\sqrt{2}m\Omega_{\rm RF}\hat\rho^2}.
\end{equation}
The axial potential then acts to ``defocus'' the radial confinement, with an
increased axial frequency leading to a reduced radial. This equal defocussing of
the two radials is due to the assumed electrode symmetry of the idealised
potential in equation~\ref{eqn:potential}. If the static potential acts
asymmetrically on the two radial dimensions, this breaks the degeneracy of the
radial modes. In this thesis, we enforce a strong DC radial asymmetry to ensure
the two radial modes are well frequency separated (electrode configuration
details in~\S~\ref{sec:Trap DC Voltages}).

For $N$ ions there are $3N$ common secular motional modes, with mode frequencies
and ion spacing set by the pseudopotential and the Coulomb repulsion between
ions~\cite{james_quantum_1998}.

\subsection{The Qumode}
\label{sec:qumode}

This secular motion is well approximated by simple harmonic oscillations for amplitudes small compared to the length scale of any anharmonicities in the pseudopotential. 
A quantised description of the oscillator is required when the motion is suitably damped to a temperature of order the mode frequency, $T\sim \hbar \omega_x/k_B$, where $\omega_x$ is the mode frequency, and $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant.
The qumode is then given by,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm osc} = \frac{1}{2m}\hat p^2 + \frac{1}{2}m\omega_x^2\hat x^2,
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the ion mass, and $\hat x$, $\hat p$ are the position and momentum operators respectively. A description of the oscillator state may be given in phase space using the $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ operators.

In the phonon, or Fock-state, representation, the creation and annihilation operators, $\hat a^\dagger$ and $\hat a$,
are related to this picture by,
\begin{align}
    \label{eqn:x_ladder}
    \hat x &= x_0 (\hat a^\dagger + \hat a),\\
    \hat p &= ip_0 (\hat a^\dagger - \hat a),
\end{align}
where $x_0=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_x}}$ is the ground state wave-packet extent, and $p_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar m \omega_x}{2}}$ is the ground state momenta.
These ladder operators provide an alternative basis to describe the oscillator state. They act on the oscillator energy levels as,
\begin{equation}
    \hat a^\dagger\ket{n} = \sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1},~~~~ \hat a\ket{n} = \sqrt{n}\ket{n-1},
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the Fock state, and the eigenvalue of the number operator, $\hat n\ket{n}=n\ket{n}$ with $\hat n = \hat a^\dagger \hat a$.
The oscillator Hamiltonian is then given succinctly by,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm osc} = \hbar \omega_x \left(\hat n + \frac{1}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
In this thesis, we will denote an oscillators state either in this Fock-state basis, where,
$\ket{n}=\frac{\hat a^{\dagger n}}{\sqrt{n!}} \ket{0}$, or in a coherent-state basis, $\ket{\beta} = \hat D(\beta)\ket{0}$, where $\hat D(\beta)$ is the displacement operator which we shall explore in the following section.

\subsection{Driving Motion}
\label{sec:driving_motion}

We now consider driving the qumode by a near-resonant spatially uniform electric field,
\begin{equation}
    E(t) = E_0  \cos(\omega t + \phi),
\end{equation}
with the Hamiltonian given by,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H(t) = \hat H_{\rm osc} + \hat H_I, ~~~~~~ \hat H_I = -e\hat xE(t),
\end{equation}
where $+e$ is the ion charge. 
Going into the interaction picture with respect to $\hat H_{\rm osc}$, we find,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde H_I = -\hbar \Omega_D\cos(\omega t + \phi) (\hat a^\dagger e^{i\omega_x t}+\hat ae^{-i\omega_x t}),
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_D = \frac{ex_0E_0}{\hbar}$. Expanding the oscillating field and assuming $\omega+ \omega_x \gg \Omega_D$, the fast oscillating terms may be neglected by the rotating-wave approximation yielding,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:displace_H}
    \tilde H_I \approx \hat H_{\rm D} := -\frac{\hbar \Omega_D}{2}(\hat a^\dagger e^{-i(\delta t+\phi)}+\hat ae^{i(\delta t+\phi)}),
\end{equation}
where $\delta = \omega - \omega_x$.

In the case of resonant driving, $\delta = 0$, the Hamiltonian becomes time-independent, and we recover the propagator,
\begin{align}
    \hat D(\beta) := \hat U(t) &= e^{i\frac{\Omega_D t}{2}(\hat a^\dagger e^{-i\phi}+\hat a e^{i\phi})},\\
    &= e^{\beta \hat a^\dagger - \beta^*\hat a},
\end{align}
recognising the second expression as the conventional displacement operator,
$\hat D(\beta)$, with $\beta = \frac{\Omega_D t}{2}ie^{-i\phi}$. The
displacement operator is used in the coherent state representation of the
oscillator, $\ket{\beta} = \hat D(\beta)\ket{0}$, where $\ket{\beta}$ is the eigenstate of the annihilation operator, $\hat a\ket{\beta} = \beta\ket{\beta}$.  The action of the displacement
operator may be visualised in phase space, shown for resonance in
Figure~\ref{fig:displacement}~(a).

Considering the off-resonant case, $\delta \neq 0$, the Hamiltonian $\tilde H_I$ 
does not self-commute over time, generally $[\hat H_D(t_1), \hat H_D(t_2)]\neq 0$. The resulting dynamics are derived by the Magnus expansion in Appendix~\ref{app:geometric_phase}, yielding the effective unitary at time $T$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:displacement}
    \hat U(T) \approx \hat D(\beta(T))e^{-i\Phi(T)}.
\end{equation}
The first term is the displacement operator, the second is an accumulation of global geometric phase.
The displacement parameter $\beta$ is given by,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:beta}
    \beta(T) = \frac{\Omega_D}{\delta}\sin\left(\frac{\delta T}{2}\right)ie^{-i(\delta T/2 + \phi)}.
\end{equation}
with a trajectory tracing a circle of radius $\frac{\Omega_D}{2|\delta|}$, returning to the
initial motional state with periodicity $T_n = \frac{2\pi n}{|\delta|}$, for integer $n$.
The phase space dynamics for the off-resonant displacement is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:displacement}~(b).

The geometric phase term, originating from the second
order term in the Magnus expansion, is given by,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:phi}
    \Phi(T) = \frac{\pi\Omega_D^2}{4\delta}\left(T-\frac{\sin(\delta t)}{\delta}\right).
\end{equation}
Considering the loop-closure times, $T_n$, the oscillatory term of the phase disappears, leaving,
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(T_n) = \frac{n\pi\Omega_D^2}{2\delta^2}.
\end{equation}
In this example, the geometric phase is globally acting, and as such can be safely ignored. This geometric phase is utilised spin-dependently in the final section of this chapter to induce two-qubit entanglement via a shared motional mode.

\section{Spin-Motion}
\label{sec:Motional Coupling}

In this section, we explore light-matter interactions that couple the spin and motional subsystems.
Reconsider the spin transition Hamiltonian, $\tilde H_I$, in equation~\ref{eqn:light_matter}, now including quantised secular ion motion in the $x$-direction given by equation~\ref{eqn:x_ladder}, around an equilibrium position, $\vec R = R_{\rm eq} + x_0(\hat a^\dagger + \hat a)\hat{e_x}$, where $\hat e_x$ is the $x$ unit vector.
The interaction Hamiltonian is then given by\footnote{Keeping in mind this Hamiltonian is already in the interaction picture with respect to the spin, $\hat H_s$, and is neglecting fast oscillating terms},
\begin{equation}
    \tilde H_I \approx \hat H_{\rm osc} + \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\left(\hat\sigma_+ e^{i\eta(\hat a^\dagger+\hat a )}e^{-i(\delta t - \phi)} + {\rm h.c.} \right),
\end{equation}
where the equilibrium position has been absorbed into the global phase, and we
have defined $\eta = |\vec k|x_0\cos(\gamma)$, the Lamb-Dicke parameter, with $\gamma$
being the angle between the $k$-vector of optical field and the mode vector $\hat e_x$. 
The Lamb-Dicke parameter relates the excitation wavelength to the extent
of the ground-state oscillator wavefunction, $x_0$. The parameter also quantifies the
strength of the coupling between the incident light and the ion's motion; it may be expressed as the ratio of photon recoil energy, $E_R=\frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m}$, to oscillator energy,
$E_{\rm osc}=\hbar\omega_x$, as $\eta^2 = \frac{E_R}{E_{\rm osc}}$.

For the wavelength (729-nm) and mode frequencies ($\approx 3~\unit{\mega\hertz}$) considered in this thesis, the Lamb-Dicke parameter is of order $\eta \approx 0.05$.

To further analyse the dynamics, the interaction Hamiltonian is transformed to the frame rotating with the oscillator energy, $\hat H_{\rm osc}$, yielding,
\begin{equation}
    \vardbtilde H_I \approx \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\hat\sigma_+ e^{i\eta(\hat a^\dagger e^{i\omega_x t}+\hat a e^{-i\omega_x t} )}e^{-i(\delta t - \phi)} + {\rm h.c.} ~~~.
\end{equation}


\subsection{Resolved Sideband Interactions}
\label{sec:resolved_sidebands}

\begin{figure}
    \begin{center}
    \noindent\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{
        figures/pdf_figure/ch2/ladder.pdf
        }
    \end{center}
    \caption{
        In the resolved sideband regime the hybrid qubit-qumode system appears as a ladder of possible states. The carrier (green) induces spin flips, $\ket{\da,n}\leftrightarrow\ket{\ua,n}$, while the sideband interactions couples the spin and neighbouring oscillator levels. For the red-sideband (red) the coupled states are $\ket{\da,n}\leftrightarrow\ket{\ua,n-1}$, and for the blue-sideband (blue) they are $\ket{\da,n}\leftrightarrow\ket{\ua,n+1}$.
        }
    \label{fig:sidebands}
\end{figure}

The resolved motional sideband regime is well approximated when the motional wavefunction extent $\sqrt{\langle \hat x^2\rangle}$ is small compared to the optical wavelength, $\eta^2(2\hat n+1)\ll~1$. In this ``Lamb-Dicke'' regime, the interaction Hamiltonian can be expand in terms of $\eta$ as (here only to first order), 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:LD_regime}
 \hat H_{\rm LD} = \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\hat\sigma_+ \left(\hat I+ i\eta(\hat a^\dagger e^{i\omega_x t}+\hat a e^{-i\omega_x t} ) + O(\eta^2)\right)e^{-i(\delta t - \phi)} + {\rm h.c.} ,
\end{equation}
where $\hat I$ is the identity operator of the qumode.

Three resonant regimes are then apparent:\\
Taking $\delta = 0$, and assuming $\eta\Omega \ll \omega_x$ (adiabatic driving) the non-motional term is brought into resonance, and terms linear in $\eta$ may be neglected. This ``carrier'' interaction is identical (up to first order) to the Hamiltonian considered above for Rabi oscillations.

The two remaining resonance conditions are termed sidebands, with $\delta = +\omega_x + \Delta $, and $\delta = -\omega_x - \Delta$, with $\Delta \ll \omega_x$, yielding the blue-sideband (BSB) and red-sideband (RSB) respectively.
To ensure off-resonant carrier excitations are negligible, it is required that $\Omega \ll \omega_x$.
In this case, the sideband Hamiltonians are written as,
\begin{align}
    \hat H_{\rm BSB} &= \frac{i\hbar \eta\Omega}{2}\left( \hat\sigma_+\hat a^\dagger e^{-i(\Delta t-\phi)} - \hat\sigma_-\hat a e^{i(\Delta t -\phi)}\right),\\
    \hat H_{\rm RSB} &= \frac{i\hbar \eta\Omega}{2}\left(\hat\sigma_+\hat a e^{i(\Delta t + \phi)} - \hat\sigma_-\hat a^\dagger e^{-i(\Delta t +\phi)}\right),
\end{align}
with the subscript labelling either BSB or RSB interactions.
When $\Delta = 0$, the RSB is the Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian
familiar from cavity QED. It describes the coherent exchange of a single quantum of energy between a two-level system
and a bosonic mode, coupling the two states $\ket{\da,n}\leftrightarrow\ket{\ua,n-1}$. The resulting dynamic evolution is,
\begin{equation}
R_{\rm RSB}(\Omega^{(n)}_{\rm RSB}t) :=e^{-i\hat H_{\rm RSB}t/2}\ket{\da, n} = \cos\left(\frac{\Omega^{(n)}_{\rm RSB}t}{2}\right)\ket{\da,n} + \sin\left(\frac{\Omega^{(n)}_{\rm RSB}t}{2}\right)\ket{\ua,n-1},
\end{equation}
where we have assumed $\phi=0$, and $\Omega^{(n)}_{\rm RSB} = \sqrt{n}\eta\Omega$ is the $n$-dependent modified RSB Rabi frequency.

The resonant BSB is described by the anti-Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian, corresponding to the simultaneous creation or annihilation of pairs of quanta, akin to pair-creation in two-mode squeezing interactions. This coherently couples the states $\ket{\da,n}\leftrightarrow\ket{\ua,n+1}$, with the dynamic evolution given by,
\begin{equation}
R_{\rm BSB}(\Omega^{(n)}_{\rm BSB}t) := e^{-i\hat H_{\rm BSB}t/2}\ket{\da, n} = \cos\left(\frac{\Omega^{(n)}_{\rm BSB}t}{2}\right)\ket{\da,n} + \sin\left(\frac{\Omega^{(n)}_{\rm BSB}t}{2}\right)\ket{\ua,n+1},
\end{equation}
where $\Omega^{(n)}_{\rm BSB} = \sqrt{n+1}\eta\Omega$ is the $n$-dependent modified BSB Rabi-frequency.

The resulting resolved sideband structure is shown schematically in
Figure~\ref{fig:sidebands} with the carrier, RSB, and BSB transitions
highlighted for one state, $\ket{\da, 1}$. In contrast to the coherent
displacements in~\S~\ref{sec:driving_motion}, these sideband interactions offer
a method for coherent control in the discrete Fock-state basis, selectively
coupling quantised states of the oscillator.
They also provide a method to directly entangle the spin and motional subsystems of the hybrid system.


\subsection{The Spin-Dependent Force}
\label{sec:SDF}

Here we introduce a fundamental interaction used throughout this thesis --- the spin-dependent force, or SDF.
The SDF is used in this thesis for three applications: For two-qubit entanglement via a shared motional mode (\S~\ref{sec:Geometric Phase Gates} and~\S~\ref{sec:Two-Qubit Entangling Gates}), for motional state tomography (\S~\ref{sec:tomography}), and for driving effective nonlinear bosonic interactions (\S~\ref{sec:two-sdf}).

As the name suggests, we consider displacements of the form given in equation~\ref{eqn:displace_H}, with conditioning dependent on an ancillary qubit state, described by the Hamiltonian,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:SDF}
    \hat H_{\rm SDF} = \hat\sigma_{\phi_s}\hat H_{\rm D},
\end{equation}
with the spin-conditioning in the $xy$-plane, given by spin phase, $\phi_s$.

In this section, we analyse how to generate an effective SDF using a combination of RSB and BSB fields.
In the next section we discuss using the SDF and the resulting spin-dependent geometric phase to realise two-qubit entangling gates.\\

In this thesis, the SDF is realised by the optical Mølmer-Sørensen (MS) scheme~\cite{sorensen_entanglement_2000} via a bichromatic laser field. Bichromatic refers to the simultaneous
application of two frequency tones. The tones are set symmetrically detuned around the carrier transition, $\omega_1 = \omega_0 + \omega_x + \Delta$, $\omega_2 = \omega_0 - \omega_x - \Delta$. The bichromatic Hamiltonian is then given by,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm bi}(t) = \hat H_{\rm RSB}(t) + \hat H_{\rm BSB}(t).
\end{equation}
The parameters for the sidebands are labelled with subscript $r$, $b$, for RSB and BSB respectively.
To realise the SDF, the Rabi frequencies are balanced, $\Omega_r = \Omega_b = \Omega$.
Expanding the Hamiltonian, and grouping spin and motional terms, we find,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm bi} = \hat\sigma_{\phi_s}\frac{\hbar \Omega_{\rm SDF}}{2} \left(\hat a^\dagger e^{-i(\Delta t + \phi_m)} + \hat a e^{i(\Delta t +\phi_m)}\right),
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to equation~\ref{eqn:SDF} with $\Omega_D \rightarrow \Omega_{\rm SDF} = \eta\Omega$, spin phase given by $\phi_s = \frac{\phi_b + \phi_r + \pi}{2}$, and a motional phase defined as $\phi_m = \frac{\phi_r - \phi_b}{2}$.

\subsection{Geometric Phase Gates}
\label{sec:Geometric Phase Gates}

To generate two-qubit entanglement we utilise the ``MS gate'' method~\cite{molmer_multiparticle_1999}: the SDF is applied simultaneously to two qubits, and a common motional mode. The MS gate
relies on the spin-dependent geometric phase accumulated during the motional
displacement~\cite{ozeri_tutorial_2011}. To ensure there is no remaining spin-motion entanglement the SDF is driven off-resonantly by $\Delta$ for an integer loop duration $T_n$, as described in~\S~\ref{sec:driving_motion}, such that the motional state returns to the initial state no matter the two-qubit spin state.

Here we assume the spin phase, $\phi_s = 0$, so the SDF is conditioned on $\hat \sigma_x$, and to simplify the analysis we assume the motional phase, $\phi_m = 0$.
If applied to two qubits, the spin conditioning is the collective spin operator $\hat S_{x}=\hat\sigma_x\otimes \hat I + \hat I\otimes\hat\sigma_x$, yielding the two-qubit and single-qumode Hamiltonian,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm MS}(t) = \frac{\hbar \Omega_{\rm SDF}}{2}\hat S_{x}(\hat a^\dagger e^{-i\Delta t}+\hat a e^{i\Delta t}).
\end{equation}
As with the analysis of the geometric phase of the non-spin-conditioned displacement, we analyse this time dependent Hamiltonian by the Magnus expansion to find,
\begin{equation}
    \hat U_{\rm MS}(t) = \hat D(\beta(t)\hat S_{x})e^{-i\Phi(t)\hat S_{x}^2},
\end{equation}
where both $\beta$ and $\phi$ are the same as in equations~\ref{eqn:beta} and~\ref{eqn:phi}, respectively, but with $\delta\rightarrow\Delta$ and $\Omega_D\rightarrow\Omega_{\rm SDF}$.
% \begin{equation}
%     \beta(t) = \frac{\Omega_{\rm SDF}}{\delta_g}\sin\left(\frac{\delta_g t}{2}\right)ie^{-i(\delta_g t/2)},
% \end{equation}
% \begin{equation}
%     \Phi(t) = \frac{\Omega_{\rm SDF}^2}{4\delta_g}\left(t-\frac{\sin(\delta_g t)}{\delta_g}\right).
% \end{equation}
Considering the loop closure time $T_n = \frac{2\pi n}{|\delta|}$, the dynamics reduce to the time-independent effective Hamiltonian, with no motional component,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{\rm eff} = \frac{\pi\hbar\Omega_{\rm SDF}^2}{4\Delta}\hat S_x^2.
\end{equation}
Taking $\hat S_x^2 = 2(\hat I + \sigma_x\otimes\sigma_x)$, the spin-conditioning has a global and two-body phase component. The global phase may be ignored, leaving the final propagator as,
\begin{equation}
    R_{xx}(\theta) := e^{-i\theta\hat\sigma_x\otimes\hat\sigma_x/2},
\end{equation}
with angle,
\begin{equation}
\theta = \frac{\Omega_{\rm SDF}^2T_n}{\Delta}.
\end{equation}
As such, the phase accumulated is independent of the initial motional state (assuming we are in the resolved sideband regime), meaning the gate is robust to imperfect cooling of the participating motional mode.

The phase space dynamics for this entangling gate are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:ms_trajectories}. The initial state is expressed in the
$x$-basis as $\ket{\da\da}=\frac{1}{2}(\ket{++}+\ket{+-}+\ket{-+}+\ket{--})$,
with the trajectories associated with $\ket{++}$ and $\ket{--}$ tracing closed
loops in phase space, while spin-component 
$\ket{+-}$ and $\ket{-+}$ remain at the origin.

Starting from the initial separable spin state $\ket{\da\da}$, application of $\hat R_{xx}(\pi/2)$ generates the maximally entangled two-qubit Bell state,
\begin{equation}
    \hat R_{xx}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) \ket{\da\da} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\da\da}-i\ket{\ua\ua}).
\end{equation}
Setting $\theta = \pi/2$ and ensuring the duration is an integer number of loop periods restricts the gate detuning to $\Delta = \delta_g$ and $T = T_n$ with,
\begin{align}
    \label{eqn:nloop}
\delta_g &= 2\sqrt{n}\Omega_{\rm SDF},\\
T_n &= \frac{\pi\sqrt{n}}{\Omega_{\rm SDF}}.
\end{align}
